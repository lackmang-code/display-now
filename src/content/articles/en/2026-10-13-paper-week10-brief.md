From Monday, October 5 to Sunday, October 11, 2026, **1,192** papers published in 28 major journals were checked and narrowed to 87 first-round candidates, and from those, 39 that bear directly on displays were picked by hand. One of them, a near-infrared radical OLED, is covered as a separate in-depth article, and **18** are introduced here.

The first axis of this window is **where efficiency and lifetime part ways**. Item 1 shows, within a single device family, that the most efficient composition is not the most stable one, and item 2 summarizes how green perovskites have entered the 180,000-hour lifetime range. The in-depth article runs along the same line.

The second axis is **blue**. Blue papers arrived from the quantum-dot, perovskite and organic-emitter sides (items 3, 4 and 5). With red and green also present, this window alone fills out RGB (items 2 and 6).

And **liquid crystals and reflective displays** returned with four papers after a long gap (items 9 to 12). For a display magazine, this is an axis that had been empty.

## 1. A red phosphorescent OLED whose most efficient and most stable compositions differed

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.07 · Wei He, Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School, Tsinghua University, China, and 8 others*

The team built the hole-blocking layer of a red phosphorescent OLED from a blend of two materials and measured efficiency and lifetime separately while varying the blend ratio. The composition series co-deposits ANT-BIZ, which has high mobility, with B4PyMPM, which has a deep highest occupied molecular orbital.

**Efficiency rose monotonically with the ANT-BIZ ratio. Lifetime, however, was longest at an intermediate composition.** The 75% ANT-BIZ composition had a higher maximum external quantum efficiency, but its measured lifetime (LT95) was **17% shorter** than that of the 50% composition. The energy levels of the two compositions are nearly identical.

The team traced the difference to **charge accumulating at the interface**. Static energy-level alignment does not explain it; what separates the lifetimes is that the amount of interfacial charge storage changes with composition. The result is that **choosing a composition on efficiency alone gives up lifetime**.

## 2. Green perovskite LEDs entering the 180,000-hour lifetime range

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.07 · Eng Liang Lim, Institute of Luminescent Materials and Information Displays, Xiamen University, China, and 3 others*

Inorganic cesium lead bromide (CsPbBr₃) has better thermal and chemical stability than perovskites that use organic cations, and has long been named as an emissive-layer candidate. This review summarizes how far 3D CsPbBr₃ devices with polycrystalline green emissive layers have come.

The numbers stand out. **Maximum external quantum efficiency is 32.1%**, and the **operating lifetime is 185,600 hours** at an initial luminance of 100cd/m². The efficiency is at the level of phosphorescent OLEDs, and the lifetime exceeds 20 years under that condition.

These values, however, are **the best the field has reached**, and the abstract alone does not show whether a single device achieved both efficiency and lifetime together. They should be read with the caveat that the efficiency record and the lifetime record may belong to different devices. What the review also points out is reproducibility, scaling to large areas, and the absence of blue.

## 3. A fluorinated monolayer that changed hole injection in deep-blue QLEDs

*Laser &amp; Photonics Reviews · 10.09 · Xinyu Zhang, Hebei Normal University for Nationalities, China, and 4 others*

What holds back blue quantum-dot LEDs is **holes not getting in well enough**. When the numbers of electrons and holes do not match, recombination tilts to one side and efficiency drops.

The team laid a **fluorinated self-assembled monolayer** on top of a nickel oxide hole injection layer. The molecules create a dipole at the interface that pushes the valence band maximum of nickel oxide deeper, bringing its energy level that much closer into line with the quantum dots.

Among several modifiers, **pentafluorobenzylphosphonic acid (F₅BnPA)** created the strongest dipole. It is an approach that fixes charge balance with a single interfacial layer, and it differs from this week's in-depth article (radical OLEDs), which tackles the same problem through the host.

## 4. Pure-blue perovskite quantum dots made by laying ligands down again with heat

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.08 · Furong Yu, Institute of Luminescent Materials and Information Displays, Xiamen University, China, and 5 others*

Producing pure blue with perovskite quantum dots requires mixing bromine and chlorine, but during synthesis **long-chain organic ligands attach in a disordered way and many surface defects form.** Under applied voltage, radiative recombination does not occur readily, so efficiency and color purity are cut together.

The team's method is **to rearrange and densify the ligands by heat treatment**. On the surface of CsPbBrₓCl₃₋ₓ quantum dots, the long chains dynamically reorganize and their density rises.

Because it treats the dots after synthesis rather than changing the synthesis, it is relatively easy to slot into a process. Read together with item 3, it shows that the bottleneck in blue lies on both sides, **injection (item 3) and surface (item 4)**.

## 5. Sky-blue TADF emitters with four fluorene arms

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.07 · Aliaksei Ivanchanka, Department of Polymer Chemistry and Technology, Kaunas University of Technology, Lithuania, and 6 others*

The team reported two emitters (FABP and EFABP, which carries ethyl groups) with **four fluorenes attached like arms** to a donor-acceptor-donor structure. They are thermally activated delayed fluorescence materials in the sky-blue region.

What stands out is the observation that **several emissive states coexist, and annealing redistributes their weights**. Calculations with constrained dihedral angles showed that **structural flexibility strongly reshapes the excited-state landscape.**

High hole mobility was also reported. If the emitter also handles transport, the stack can be reduced, which places this paper alongside this week's in-depth article and its point that "charge and emission cannot be chosen separately."

## 6. Pure-red perovskite LEDs with quantum wells stood upright

*Advanced Materials · 10.04 · Guan-Jie Ding, University of Science and Technology of China (USTC), Hefei, and 10 others*

Quasi-2D perovskites have a quantum-well structure with large cations inserted, which favors pure-red emission. The problem was that **every quasi-2D emitter reported so far grows lying flat on a low-mobility organic electron transport layer**. Carriers do not get in well.

The team used branched guanidinium and aprotic phosphonium cations to induce the emissive layer to **grow vertically**. Beneath it they placed ZnMgO, a highly conductive inorganic electron transport layer.

It is a design that aligns the orientation of the quantum wells with the direction from which carriers arrive. Together with item 2 (green) and items 3 to 5 (blue), this window alone fills out RGB.

## 7. Light-extraction microstructures for blue top-emitting OLEDs found by reinforcement learning

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.09 · Zi-Run Zhao, Key Laboratory of Luminescence and Optical Information, Ministry of Education, Beijing Jiaotong University, China, and 7 others*

Extracting trapped light from blue top-emitting OLEDs requires **optical structures that redistribute the internal modes**. But geometry and composition are entangled, so changing conditions by hand is inefficient.

The team optimized an array of titanium dioxide/NOA61 micropillars with a **deep Q-network (DQN)**. Pillar radius, array period and titanium dioxide fill ratio are handled **together as coupled variables**.

For readers of this issue, it is **the direct sequel to the issue 8 in-depth article** ([Gentler Corrugation Boosts Top-Emitting OLED Outcoupling](/en/article/2026-10-06-te-oled-gentle-corrugation-outcoupling)). There, the key was how little the slope of the corrugation disturbed the cavity; this paper has reinforcement learning, rather than people, sweep that design space.

## 8. 20.8% external quantum efficiency in red micro-LEDs

*ACS Photonics · 10.07 · Roark Chao, University of California, USA, and 9 others*

Producing red with indium gallium nitride requires a lot of indium, and that lowers radiative efficiency. This is why red micro-LEDs have lagged behind blue and green.

The team **surrounded the sidewalls with an annular distributed Bragg reflector (DBR)** to raise light extraction in devices emitting at 619 nanometers. Fully packaged devices reached a **maximum external quantum efficiency of 20.8% and a wall-plug efficiency of 13.2%**.

The authors describe this as **the highest performance among planar (not using a V-defect process) gallium nitride red micro-LEDs** on sapphire substrates. Since red has been the gate in the push to produce full color from a single epitaxial stack, it is a number worth watching.

## 9. Cholesteric reflective displays rewritten without a photomask

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.07 · Yongbin Xing, College of Materials Science and Engineering, Beijing University of Chemical Technology, China, and 5 others*

Oblique helicoidal cholesteric liquid crystals are a reflective-display material whose reflected color changes with voltage. The obstacles were that **the patterning process is complex and the operation is static**.

The team built an optically and electrically co-addressed device that is **fully reconfigurable without a photomask**. A chiral azobenzene photoswitch (Azo-MX) is added so that information is **written with 520-nanometer visible light and erased by heating**.

Combining digital optical addressing with thermal erasure, it writes and erases information quickly. Reflective displays do not emit their own light and use almost no power, yet among previously published in-depth articles this axis had only [a single piece on liquid-crystal gels](/en/article/2026-09-29-lc-gel-temperature-capacitor).

## 10. A structural-color metafabric that changes color with heat

*Small · 10.07 · Zhi-Ting Bian, National Engineering Laboratory for Modern Silk, Soochow University, China, and 5 others*

There is demand for outdoor textiles that **regulate temperature on their own while also looking good**. Black-white switching to manage solar heat has been proposed, but it conflicts optically with the conventional approach of producing color with dyes.

What the team made is a **thermochromic structural-color metafabric**. It handles light in three regions at once, visible, solar and mid-infrared, achieving both temperature self-regulation and color expression.

The point to watch from the display side is that **color comes from structure rather than pigment**. Structural color is an axis with no previously published in-depth article, and it shares roots with reflective, low-power display devices.

## 11. A liquid-crystal lens that switches between two modes by voltage

*Optics Express · 10.05 · Weiping Ding and 5 others*

Bright-field imaging and phase-contrast imaging are two complementary methods in optical microscopy and optical analog computing. Devices that perform both **alternately or simultaneously** are a research topic, but existing dual-mode devices had low integration, stiff switching and complex fabrication.

The team presented an electrically driven dual-function liquid-crystal lens made by **direct laser writing**. A focusing lens and a lens imparting spiral phase are **stacked vertically**, and voltage switches the mode.

Because it uses liquid crystals to write the phase of light spatially, on the display side it touches varifocal optics and near-eye displays.

## 12. Perovskite grown in one direction inside a liquid-crystalline polymer template

*ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces · 10.07 · Tarachand Maibam, Jeonbuk National University, and 3 others*

Metal halide perovskite nanocrystals have good optical properties, but **aligning their orientation neatly** is the prerequisite for anisotropic optical functions.

The team designed a **lyotropic liquid-crystalline polymer template** that induces both the shape anisotropy and the crystallographic orientation of CsPbBr₃ nanocrystals. Shear-aligned bundles of hexagonally ordered polymer act as an impermeable template, guiding the spatial arrangement of the perovskite phase on a macroscopic scale.

The result is **linearly polarized emission**. Instead of discarding half the light with a polarizer, it moves toward creating polarization at the emission stage, and it is Korean research.

## 13. A 986-nanometer perovskite LED that turns one photon into two

*ACS Nano · 10.05 · Yuqi Wang, Jilin University, China, and 10 others*

Quasi-2D metal halide perovskites doped with ytterbium ions are a platform that emits in the near infrared beyond 950 nanometers. **Quantum cutting** takes place, in which one absorbed photon is split into two infrared photons.

The team tuned the phase distribution of the emissive layer and carrier transport in the hole transport layer **together**. Using a fluorinated aromatic spacer and partial formamidinium substitution, they recorded an **external quantum efficiency of 12.1%** at 986 nanometers.

Set next to this week's in-depth article (an 804-nanometer radical OLED, EQE 8.1%), it forms **two routes to near-infrared emission**. One is doublet emission from organic radicals; the other is quantum cutting in inorganic perovskites.

## 14. TADF that switches off as the donor gets stronger

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.07 · Anthony D'Aléo, IPCMS, CNRS Strasbourg, France, and 13 others · with OPERA, Kyushu University*

Obtaining efficient TADF in the deep red and near infrared is still difficult. Curcuminoid boron difluoride (CurcBF₂) dyes have a track record in near-infrared OLEDs, using TADF that draws on vibronic and spin-orbit coupling.

The team made derivatives with varying donor strength and followed them spectroscopically. **Weak to moderate donors produce charge-transfer emission and thin-film TADF exciplexes, while strong donors produce TADF from the molecule itself. Going stronger still shifts the structure toward polymethine, and TADF is no longer detected at all.**

**At that point the emission linewidth narrows.** It broadens from 2,400cm⁻¹ to 3,300cm⁻¹, then shrinks to 1,800cm⁻¹ and 970cm⁻¹ once the dye becomes polymethine. The core of this paper is that the narrower linewidth is not a good sign but **a sign that TADF has died**.

## 15. Temperature dependence of a transistor combining a ferroelectric polymer with an oxide semiconductor

*ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces · 10.05 · Chenru Zhao, Fudan University, China, and 8 others*

Ferroelectric thin-film transistors are candidates for nonvolatile memory and neuromorphic devices. Temperature-dependent characteristics reveal how polarization modulates carrier transport, but **conventional ferroelectrics have high Curie temperatures**, so measuring at high temperature also changes the semiconductor's own properties, making it hard to isolate the share due to polarization.

The team combined **P(VDF-TrFE)**, which has a low Curie temperature, with an indium zinc oxide (IZO) channel, separating the contribution of polarization within a temperature range that does not damage the semiconductor.

Oxide TFTs are an axis with four previously published in-depth articles, but this work points toward **memory and computing rather than the backplane**.

## 16. An IGZO transistor topped with collagen that reads pressure without a gate

*Applied Physics Letters · 10.06 · Denice N. Feria, Department of Optoelectronics and Materials Technology, National Taiwan Ocean University, Taiwan, and 3 others*

Biocompatible, degradable piezoelectric materials have emerged as candidates for wearable and implantable sensors, but **pairing them with oxide transistors to read pressure over a wide range** has rarely been addressed.

The team coupled a piezoelectric collagen film to an amorphous IGZO transistor to build a **transparent, self-gated** pressure sensor. The pressure-induced voltage in the collagen directly modulates the channel, producing a pressure-dependent drain current **without any external gate bias**.

It is a case of a display backplane material crossing over to sensors, and it belongs to the same line as the earlier in-depth article [Why IGZO, a display backplane material, went to memory](/en/article/2026-08-18-dongguk-ald-igzo-vrram).

## 17. 7 Å, the length scale that governs rare-earth luminescence

*Advanced Photonics · 10.06 · Zhongmin Yang, School of Physics and Optoelectronics, South China University of Technology, China*

When designing rare-earth-doped amorphous materials, **local atomic coordination (short-range order)** has traditionally been seen as the primary determinant of luminescence properties.

The team combined **persistent homology from topology** with interpretable machine learning to quantify how structural order at each length scale connects to luminescence. The result runs against conventional wisdom. **Short-range order alone has weak predictive power; medium-range topological descriptors dominate, with a characteristic length of about 7 Å.**

At that scale, four luminescence properties are predicted consistently well, and adding longer-range information brings almost no gain. The emission linewidth is explained by **a single characteristic size of the network channels**. It gives grounds for phosphor design to move from "around the ion" to "the medium-range structure beyond it."

## 18. Two materials for making white light, perovskites and quantum dots

*Laser &amp; Photonics Reviews · 10.05 · Chao Wang and 4 others*

White emission is central to solid-state lighting and full-color displays. Phosphor-converted white LEDs have long faced **a trade-off between luminous efficacy and color rendering**, which constrains their use in human-centric lighting and wide-gamut displays.

This review puts bulk perovskite emitters and colloidal quantum dots (perovskite-based as well as conventional II-VI and III-V types) side by side. The grounds are widely tunable bandgaps, high photoluminescence quantum yields and tunable emission linewidths.

In this window, individual device papers came out scattered across blue, green and red (items 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6), and this review places those pieces on a single axis: **material strategies for making white**.
