From Monday, October 12 to Sunday, October 18, 2026, 1,391 papers published in 28 major journals were checked and narrowed to 127 first-round candidates, and from those, 83 that bear directly on displays were picked by hand. Two in-depth articles are covered separately, and 18 are introduced here.

One thing clustered noticeably in this window. **There are four papers in which what is attached where on a molecule decides performance** (items 4, 5 and 6, plus one in-depth article). The story that position, not composition, is the variable came up four times in the same week.

The second is mechanoluminescence. Besides the stretchable film covered as an in-depth article, two more papers appeared (items 7 and 8). Three materials that emit light without a power supply in a single week is a first since the magazine launched.

And **Korean research is unusually prominent.** On top of the two in-depth articles (Ajou University and DGIST / Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology, Hanyang University and Kyungpook National University), there are item 2 (ETRI, Hanbat National University and Dongjin Semichem), item 3 (Hanbat National University) and item 18 (Samsung Electronics).

## 1. A deep-blue OLED with 35.9% external quantum efficiency and a 632-hour lifetime together

*Nature Photonics · 10.12 · Tianyu Huang, Key Laboratory of Organic Optoelectronics and Molecular Engineering of the Ministry of Education, Tsinghua University, China, and 9 others · with the University of Cambridge*

In deep-blue OLEDs, it is hard to secure efficiency, roll-off and lifetime at the same time. If the excitons created electrically cannot all be used within a short enough time, all three collapse together.

The team built an emissive system with two sensitizers. A blue TADF sensitizing host with a C3-symmetric triple donor-acceptor structure goes in, and several sensitization pathways work together to achieve nearly 100% exciton utilization on a sub-microsecond timescale. The explanation is that nearly degenerate states create a cumulative effect that breaks the trade-off between fast singlet radiation and efficient triplet upconversion.

Every number is good. The color coordinate y is 0.10, the full width at half maximum 15.4 nanometers, and the maximum external quantum efficiency 35.9%, with 90% of it retained at a high current density of 297.0mA/cm². From an initial 1,000cd/m², luminance fell to 80% after 632 hours. Exciton dynamics analysis points out that the sub-microsecond sensitization pathways greatly suppressed exciton annihilation.

## 2. Patterned Bragg reflectors deposited below 100 degrees, and 3,175ppi

*npj Flexible Electronics · 10.15 · Jin-Wook Shin, Electronics and Telecommunications Research Institute (ETRI), and 16 others · with Hanbat National University and Dongjin Semichem*

The obstacle for high-resolution QD-OLED is blue leakage. Color purity and light extraction are hard to get together, and the smaller the pixels, the more the leaking blue muddies the color.

The team placed distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs) directly on top of the QD-OLED stack. The key is temperature. Silicon nitride and silicon oxide are deposited by plasma chemical vapor deposition below 100 degrees, so the organic layers beneath are not damaged.

The performance is clear-cut. Reflectance of 95.9% at 455 nanometers blocks blue while reflection is suppressed in the red and green regions. A single-step fluorine-based dry etch stands up 4-micrometer patterns with a taper angle close to 89 degrees, supporting up to 3,175ppi.

## 3. A different culprit named in the same kind of radical OLED

*ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces · 10.15 · Woonha Lee, Hanbat National University, and 10 others*

This one should be read alongside the issue 9 in-depth article. Both deal with degradation in near-infrared radical OLEDs, but the culprit differs.

This paper analyzed charge dynamics and exciton-polaron quenching (EPQ) in MADN:TTM-TPA devices. Electrical measurements including transient electroluminescence showed that the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital energy barrier inside the emitting layer is large, so electrons are strongly trapped and accumulate excessively. In other words, electrons drive the quenching.

The issue 9 in-depth article (Radical OLEDs Where Charge Balance Sets Both Efficiency and Roll-off) used Poisson and drift-diffusion simulation and magnetic field measurements to name hole-induced quenching as the main culprit of high-current roll-off. **When the device structure and material system differ, the owner of the same phenomenon changes**, a signal that "the cause of roll-off in radical OLEDs" cannot be stated as one thing.

## 4. Hyperfluorescence hosts designed with positional isomers

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.13 · Sugarmaa Altankhuyag, OPERA, Kyushu University, Japan, and 6 others · with CNRS and the University of Rennes, France, and the Chinese Academy of Sciences*

Hyperfluorescence OLEDs offer high efficiency and good color purity together, but operational stability is their weak point. Because the emitting layer has several components, charge transport and energy transfer are complex, which makes device lifetime especially sensitive to energy level alignment. Charge accumulating from misaligned frontier orbital levels is the dominant degradation pathway under electrical driving.

The team made three pure hydrocarbon hosts. They are positional isomers (1mqp, 3mqp and 4mqp) with the same terphenyl substituent attached at different positions on a spirobifluorene backbone. Triplet energies are about 2.9eV for all three and photoluminescence quantum yields exceed 92%, nearly identical, and only the highest occupied molecular orbital levels diverge, at −6.43 / −6.40 / −6.48eV.

The result has the same shape as in-depth article ② in this issue. Device efficiency is similar for all three, at an external quantum efficiency of about 25%, but lifetime differed widely. In transient electroluminescence, hosts with deeper highest occupied molecular orbitals showed a pronounced emission spike, a sign that charge was accumulating in the emitting layer. 3mqp, in which that behavior was suppressed, lasted longest (LT50 of 59.5 hours).

What catches the eye is the size of the difference. The level difference between 1mqp and 3mqp is only 0.1eV, yet lifetime spreads apart more distinctly than that. The reason 3mqp has the shallowest level also lies in its structure. A smaller dihedral angle strengthens conjugation, and the orbital spreads more widely.

In-depth article ② showed side-chain position changing chain conformation and thereby deciding photostability; here the substitution site changes the dihedral angle and thereby decides charge accumulation and lifetime. **Both are "position, not composition," and in both, efficiency or optics are the same while only lifetime diverges.**

## 5. A deep blue tuned to 465 nanometers by attaching chalcogens at the "para" position

*Laser &amp; Photonics Reviews · 10.12 · Xuewei Nie, Guangdong Basic Research Center of Excellence for Energy and Information Polymer Materials, China, and 14 others*

Blue for ultra-high-definition displays needs an emission peak within 465±3 nanometers and fast reverse intersystem crossing. Meeting both together is hard.

The team made two derivatives (para-O-BN and para-S-BN) with a para-chalcogen-π-oxygen alignment strategy. Rigid five-membered heterocycles containing oxygen and sulfur are fused to the periphery of a classic diboron MR-TADF backbone.

With extended π-conjugation and greater molecular rigidity, the two derivatives emit almost identical ultra-narrowband deep blue. As the word "para" in the strategy's name suggests, the attachment site is the design variable.

## 6. In which direction to attach indolocarbazole

*Small · 10.13 · Qiang Zhang, Wuhan National Laboratory for Optoelectronics, China, and 9 others*

Emitters meeting the BT.2020 standard are a condition for ultra-high-definition displays, but in green MR-TADF it is hard to obtain high efficiency and high color purity together.

The team's method has two layers. Combining indolocarbazole (ICz) isomer engineering with peripheral steric shielding, they made two narrowband green emitters (Mes-b-ICzBN and Mes-c-ICzBN). A rigid indolocarbazole fragment is fused in and bulky mesitylene groups are added, and the direction in which the indolocarbazole fragment extends is what separates the two emitters.

Seen together with items 4 and 5, the character of this window shows. **Where something goes is being treated as the variable more than what goes in.**

## 7. Near-infrared mechanoluminescence made recoverable by adding defects

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.15 · Yao Xiao, State Key Laboratory of Luminescent Materials and Devices, China, and 11 others*

Near-infrared mechanoluminescence can be used for biomechanical sensing and deep-tissue imaging, giving it a place distinct from visible light. The obstacle was that the mechanism of self-recovering, power-free mechanoluminescence was unclear.

The team made a family of chromium-activated mechanoluminescent phosphors through spinel microstructure-defect engineering in a centrosymmetric host. A centrosymmetric structure has no piezoelectricity, which makes mechanoluminescence difficult there, but controlling the defects opened that door.

It shares an axis with in-depth article ① but sits at a different level. That one left the emitter as it was and placed an optical layer on top to extract the light; this one designs the defects of the emitter itself.

## 8. Mechanoluminescence powered by energy handed over through contact electrification

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.15 · Yue-Fei Xiang, Key Laboratory of Optoelectronic Functional Materials and Membrane Technology, Zhuhai, China, and 10 others*

Mechanoluminescent materials are useful for stress sensing, but the field's problem was that there is no general design principle for making efficient, self-powered materials.

The team presented the Sr₃P₄O₁₃ family and explains its mechanism as energy transfer from the recombination of electron-hole pairs created by contact electrification. They pinned down that pathway using piezoresponse force microscopy together with thermoluminescence spectroscopy and triboelectric potential analysis.

The emission of the zinc sulfide film covered in this issue's in-depth article ① is also explained by an interfacial triboelectric field. The trend of mechanoluminescence being organized around a triboelectric mechanism rather than piezoelectricity is visible across all three papers.

## 9. Perovskite LEDs made brighter and longer-lasting by changing facet competition

*Laser &amp; Photonics Reviews · 10.12 · Yue Liang, Institute of Polymer Optoelectronic Materials and Devices, China, and 9 others*

The two bottlenecks of perovskite LEDs are operational stability and luminance. They are also what blocks commercialization.

The team's method is interference crystallization. Adding L-theanine to the FAPbBr₃ precursor solution lets its functional groups adsorb selectively onto the (100) facet of the perovskite, reshaping the competition among facet growth.

The result is that changing the facet orientation raised brightness, durability and response speed together. The use of an amino acid found in green tea as the additive catches the eye.

## 10. QLEDs with a single molecular layer anchored at the buried interface

*Applied Physics Letters · 10.13 · Jing-Jing Bao, Macao Institute of Materials Science and Engineering, and 6 others*

What cuts quantum-dot LED performance is holes not getting in well enough, and the causes are surface defects and energy level mismatch. The buried interface of the hole transport layer is especially a problem.

The team modified the nickel oxide surface with a carbazole-based phosphonic acid (MeO-2PACz). The molecules anchor to the nickel oxide and tidy up the interface.

It is the same spot as item 3 of the issue 9 briefing (tuning the nickel oxide interfacial dipole with a fluorinated monolayer), with a different molecule. Blue or not, a monolayer on nickel oxide appears to be becoming a common tool for attacking QLED interfaces.

## 11. A flexible frontplane that confines liquid crystal in droplets

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.13 · Ryan A. Williams, Advanced Materials and Liquid Crystal Institute graduate program, Kent State University, USA, and 5 others*

The reason liquid crystal displays have not entered the flexible market is stated plainly. When bent, the liquid crystal flows, and that flow distorts the image.

There have been attempts to stop it with polymer structures or polymer stabilization, but they complicated the process or cut performance.

The team's alternative is an inkjet-printed array of nematic liquid crystal droplets. Splitting the liquid crystal into droplets and confining them removes the space to flow in the first place. It is at the proof-of-concept stage and comes from Kent State University, a home of liquid crystal research.

## 12. A radiative cooling window that electrically plates and strips silver

*Nano Letters · 10.15 · Jingjing Mao, Northeast Forestry University, China, and 6 others · with the Shanghai Institute of Microsystem and Information Technology*

Transparent radiative cooling windows reduce cooling load while preserving daylight, but under strong sunlight their high solar transmission becomes a burden.

Existing electrochromic windows can modulate solar transmission, but it was hard to achieve strong solar blocking, radiative heat dissipation and a wide color gamut at the same time.

The team combined a planar coupled nanocavity with reversible silver electrodeposition. Plating and stripping silver electrochemically switches between transparent and opaque while also producing color. Electrochromism is an axis with no previously published in-depth article.

## 13. Liquid crystal elastomer fibers with an order parameter raised to 0.78

*Advanced Materials · 10.16 · Chang Xu, Key Laboratory of Organic Optoelectronics and Molecular Engineering of the Ministry of Education, China, and 5 others*

Liquid crystal elastomer fibers are shape-memory materials that produce large, reversible deformations. The problem is that methods for making fibers with very fast thermal response, and the factors that set actuation performance, have not yet been sorted out.

The team made highly aligned fibers with a tandem alignment strategy based on electrospinning. They raised the order parameter to 0.78 and showed excellent actuation performance under thermal driving.

## 14. A meta-display that binds the direction of fluorescence and multiplexes by angle

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.12 · Zejing Wang, School of Electronic Information, Wuhan University, China, and 6 others*

Metasurfaces handle wavefronts freely but do not work well on fluorescent emission. Fluorescence is incoherent, spreads in all directions, has no polarization and has random phase. That is why existing multiplexing strategies cannot be used as they are.

The team bound those properties through unidirectional emission control and multiplexed photoluminescence by angle. It is an emissive metasurface display that shows a different image at each viewing angle.

The issue 8 in-depth article (leaky-mode metasurfaces) was about the device creating the coherence of the light source; this one keeps using incoherent emission and binds only its direction.

## 15. Speckle-free holography that reaches the limit of diffraction efficiency

*Laser &amp; Photonics Reviews · 10.15 · Yanjun Bao, Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Nanophotonic Manipulation, China, and 5 others*

Holography carries a fundamental trade-off between diffraction efficiency and image fidelity. Removing speckle is itself hard, and doing it while pushing efficiency to the physical limit is harder still.

The team resolved that trade-off by combining a dual-layer metasurface architecture with gradient-based progressive efficiency optimization.

It is the same spot as the issue 8 in-depth article. That piece projected holograms with a halogen lamp, where speckle was averaged down by 1/√N, but insufficient spatial coherence blurred the strokes instead. This paper uses a laser and secures both speckle suppression and efficiency. It approaches the same problem from the opposite side.

## 16. Designing RGB primaries that reduce observer color differences

*Optics Express · 10.13 · Siyuan Song and 1 other*

This is the direct sequel to item 1 of the issue 8 briefing. That piece calculated that narrowband primaries widen the color gamut at the cost of colors looking different from person to person. This paper designs primaries that reduce that difference.

The method ties the two together. Using constrained computational optimization together with psychophysical white-matching experiments, the team found and validated RGB primary spectra that reduce the magnitude of observer metameric mismatch (OMM) while preserving BT.2020 gamut coverage. In simulation, the RGB primaries were modeled as Gaussians, with peak wavelength and full width at half maximum as the variables.

If color gamut and observer color difference trade off, then where to choose on that trade-off line becomes the design problem. This paper draws that line.

## 17. 1.55 gigahertz from yellow-green micro-LEDs

*Applied Physics Letters · 10.13 · Jianhui Liu, Department of Electronic Science, Xiamen University, China, and 12 others*

Yellow-green micro-LEDs are attractive as transmitters for visible-light communication, but emitting longer wavelengths requires a lot of indium, and then efficiency, optical output and modulation speed cut into one another.

The team built c-plane single-quantum-well micro-LEDs emitting near 545 nanometers, achieving milliwatt-level output and gigahertz-level small-signal modulation together at high current density. Devices with lateral sizes of 40 micrometers and 60 micrometers showed maximum external quantum efficiencies of 5.36% and 5.35%.

It extends the issue 5 in-depth article (optical communication and ToF using Micro-LED displays), and the numbers behind the trend of screens becoming communication terminals keep accumulating.

## 18. How quantum dots entered displays, a record from someone who was there

*Nature Nanotechnology · 10.13 · Eunjoo Jang, SKKU Advanced Institute of Nanotechnology (SAINT), Sungkyunkwan University (Comment)*

This is not a research paper but a Comment. The author is Dr. Eunjoo Jang, who has long been involved in commercializing quantum dots, and she traces the process while citing two of her own papers, including an indium phosphide quantum dot review (Chem. Rev. 2023).

**The point of this piece is that "a superior material did not create the industry."** The author writes that quantum dots came in not because of a scientific breakthrough but as a result of meshing with an industrial transition.

The trigger was regulation. Early liquid crystal displays made white by striking red, green and blue phosphors with mercury fluorescent lamps (254 nanometers), but the 2003 European Union Restriction of Hazardous Substances Directive (RoHS) closed that route and pushed the move to gallium nitride blue LED (450 nanometers) backlights. With the light source changed, new red and green colour conversion materials were needed, and quantum dots took that place.

The first attempt failed. It placed red and green quantum dots directly on the blue LED chip; it looked promising because it used existing packaging and reduced the amount of quantum dots, but the quantum dots could not withstand the intense photon flux and heat load near the chip. Sealing them in glass tubes was also tried (QD Vision's Color IQ, Sony's 2013 Triluminos), but residual oxygen actually accelerated degradation, and light extraction losses and cracking risk came with it.

The decisive breakthrough was separating the colour conversion layer from the chip. Making the quantum dots into a film placed at a distance lowered the photon flux they received by more than five orders of magnitude compared with earlier designs, and oxygen and moisture barrier layers could be added with existing optical film processes. After Amazon's 2013 Kindle Fire HDX, Samsung put cadmium-free quantum dot films into mass TV production from 2015.

The next stage, quantum dot colour filters (QD-CF), holds a paradox. The Lambertian emission of quantum dots widens the viewing angle, but that light is unpolarized, which breaks a structure that adjusts brightness through liquid crystal modulation. That is why polarization-management optics became newly necessary. The route of striking quantum dots with blue OLEDs required high-order tandem stacks, because blue OLEDs are at a disadvantage to blue LEDs in efficiency, lifetime and wavelength, and that complexity eroded much of the advantage originally expected.

The last gate is quantum dot electroluminescence (QD-EL), in which quantum dots emit light directly from electricity. The author names two bottlenecks. Blue emitters need small diameters and wide bandgaps, so their surfaces are unstable, the usable compositions are narrow and efficiency and lifetime trade off severely; and zinc oxide charge transport layers bring in trap-rich interfaces and time-dependent instability. She also notes the nature of the difficulty: the limiting factors cannot be traced back to any single contribution, so design rules are slow to emerge.

The story of quantum dot colour filters is not over. She sees them possibly rising again if more stable, more strongly absorbing quantum dots are paired with excitation sources such as blue micro-LEDs and suitable optical design.
