Between Monday 19 and Sunday 25 October 2026, 1,240 papers published in 28 journals were checked. Of the 118 that passed the 3-stage filter, 19 that lead toward displays were picked.

**This week brought many papers about narrowing.** Emission linewidth down to 13 nanometers, radiation angle, color-mixing distance and crosstalk between pixels were all narrowed in work that landed in the same week. Widening the color gamut requires narrowing the primaries, and cutting power requires narrowing the direction light travels. Narrowing is happening on several layers at once.

Korean research accounts for five papers: items 1, 6, 10, 11 and 14, and Samsung Display took part in item 11.

The two in-depth articles in this issue were left off this list: one on splitting a liquid crystal's phase transition temperature into steps, and one on transparent electrodes made from electrospun nanofibers.

## 1. An electrochromic display that switches visible and near-infrared together

*npj Flexible Electronics · 10.20 · Hyo Eun Jeong, KAIST and Gwangju Institute of Science and Technology (GIST), and 11 others · with Yonsei University, Chosun University, Kongju National University and MIT*

This is Korean research, and a place this magazine has not covered since its launch.

Reflective displays use almost no power and suit flexible and wearable uses, but until now their operation has been confined to the visible range. The problem the team points to is that this limit narrows where they can be used. Environments where the same display must reach both human eyes and robotic vision systems are increasing, and if only visible light is switched, a robot cannot read it.

The conducting polymer PEDOT:PSS was placed inside a dual-band Gires-Tournois resonator. Changing the polymer's complex refractive index through electrochemical doping **modulates visible and near-infrared together with a single active layer.**

The voltage range is −2.0 volts to +1.0 volts. The hue change in the visible came out at 167.2 degrees, and the reflectance change at 1270 nanometers at 38 percent. The team went as far as a flexible device driven by an active matrix.

The reuse license is non-commercial and no-derivatives, so this magazine could not cover it in depth. We recommend reading the original directly.

## 2. Quantum-dot micropillar pixels inspired by optical fiber

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.21 · Junhu Cai, Fuzhou University, China, and 10 others · with Huazhong University of Science and Technology*

Using quantum-dot color conversion on micro-LEDs brings the problem of light leaking between pixels. So black banks are raised to block the crosstalk, but those banks absorb light and cut conversion efficiency. Crosstalk and efficiency wear each other down.

The team took its cue from the core-cladding structure of optical fiber. The quantum-dot pixels are made as vertically standing pillars, and their sidewalls are wrapped in highly reflective aluminum by deposition. The absorbing bank becomes a reflecting wall.

In a planar color conversion layer, the optical path is tied to film thickness, while standing pillars free the length over which excitation light meets quantum dots from that constraint. The pillar array was made by in-situ photolithography and the sidewall metal was added by deposition.

## 3. Organic light-emitting transistors that put emission and memory in the same place

*Advanced Functional Materials · 10.23 · Jiatong Liu, Peking University Shenzhen Graduate School, China, and 8 others · with Sun Yat-sen University*

What high-resolution smartphones, wearables and near-eye VR and AR microdisplays demand is a small pixel structure with a high aperture ratio. Organic light-emitting transistors answer that demand by putting switching and emission in a single device.

The problem was that the emission state was set only by the electrical bias of the moment. Storing a state was separate from the place that emits, so a state could not be written into the pixel itself.

The team added a floating-gate structure that can trap and release charge. The stored charge controls channel transport and emission together, so a state written electrically remains as emission.

It is the same idea as the floating-gate micro-LED pixel covered in issue 5, coming from the organic side. That one was about giving non-volatility to an inorganic light emitter.

## 4. An ultra-narrowband green emitter with a 13-nanometer full width at half maximum

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.24 · Lu Liang, Jilin University, China, and 5 others*

Widening the color gamut and cutting power require narrowing the emission linewidth. The multi-resonance thermally activated delayed fluorescence family is on that path, but producing a full width at half maximum below 20 nanometers in electroluminescence while keeping efficiency has rarely worked.

This paper attaches bis-spiro structures to a C=O/N-type multi-resonance skeleton. Balancing the repulsion between the skeleton and the surrounding steric protecting groups planarizes the skeleton, and at the same time suppresses molecular vibration, narrowing the main emission band and lowering the shoulder intensity.

The full width at half maximum is 13 nanometers in toluene and 19 nanometers in the device. Reducing the shoulder does more for color purity than the full width at half maximum figure.

## 5. An MR-TADF polymer that stretches and emits narrow deep blue

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.24 · Hai Zhou, Jiangsu Ocean University, China, and 13 others · with Yancheng Institute of Technology*

Intrinsically stretchable OLEDs are the foundation devices for wearables and flexible ultra-high-definition displays. Yet no polymer had stretched while emitting narrow deep blue and keeping its color purity. The line the authors set as the benchmark is CIE y below 0.1.

The approach is in-situ free-radical hypercrosslinking. A carbonyl-nitrogen fused ring serves as the emissive core, and ortho and para styrene substitutions tune the excited states and exciton dynamics. It is then copolymerized in situ with acrylic polyurethane to form a three-dimensional elastic network.

In treating substitution position as a variable, it stands in the same place as the issue 10 in-depth article. That piece was about whether a side chain sits at para or meta deciding photostability.

## 6. A deep-blue perovskite LED that holds its color by reducing chloride vacancies

*ACS Photonics · 10.23 · Hyojun Kim, KAIST, and 8 others · with POSTECH*

In perovskite light-emitting devices, deep blue lags red and green in efficiency and operational stability. Among the routes to deep blue, mixed halides are the most practical, but they carry a structural weakness.

The ionic interaction between chloride and lead is weak, so chloride vacancies form easily. Those vacancies become the starting points of halide segregation, and under applied voltage the spectrum wavers. Modifying the precursor with ligands can partly suppress vacancy formation, but ligands escape during crystallization, making complete elimination difficult.

What the team offers is sequential stabilization, a method that blocks the places where vacancies form in separate steps.

## 7. A cesium lead bromide LED that separates two phases in time

*Advanced Materials · 10.22 · Jike Zhang, University of Macau, and 17 others · with Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications, Sichuan University and Sun Yat-sen University*

Cesium lead bromide is a strong candidate for perovskite light-emitting devices, but the zero-dimensional Cs₄PbBr₆ phase tags along with the crystallization of three-dimensional CsPbBr₃. When the two crystallize mixed together, film quality drops and defect density rises. It is where efficiency gets cut on the way to mass production.

Adding 3-carboxyphenylphosphonic acid controlled the crystallization rates of the two phases separately. When the additive interacts strongly with the precursor and delays the early formation of CsPbBr₃, the zero-dimensional and three-dimensional phases crystallize one after the other, separated in time.

A Nature Photonics paper from the same window also improves perovskite light-emitting devices by controlling crystallization rate. This one separates phases with an additive; that one steers the crystallization path in a thermal evaporation process.

## 8. A near-infrared perovskite LED with pixels patterned by thermal evaporation

*Nature Photonics · 10.23 · Yangyu Liu, Nanjing University, China, and 16 others · with Nanjing Tech University and Linköping University, Sweden*

Thermal evaporation favors large-area mass production and pixel patterning, but evaporated perovskite devices have performed below solution-processed ones. There was no way to manage crystallization during vacuum deposition.

A bis-amide additive suppresses premature phase formation during deposition and slows the crystallization rate during post-annealing. At 805-nanometer emission, the external quantum efficiency was 25.0 percent, with a lifetime of 23 hours at 100 milliamperes per square centimeter.

What deserves attention comes next. **805-nanometer and 759-nanometer devices were built monolithically on the same substrate** to produce two colors, demonstrated in dual-wavelength wearable pulse oximetry. It is a place where pixels divided by evaporation were actually shown.

## 9. A superhydrophobic quantum-dot film that keeps emitting at 800 percent stretch

*Advanced Materials · 10.24 · Jianqiang Xiao, Central South University, China, and 6 others*

The demands on perovskite quantum-dot films for flexible optics have grown. Good emission alone is not enough; they must withstand large deformation, liquid penetration and long-term use together.

Combining dual-phase engineering with replica molding and femtosecond laser direct writing, the team made a biomimetic superhydrophobic surface and precipitated the quantum dots in a controlled way. At 800 percent tensile strain, the film kept more than 52 percent of its initial emission intensity and a water contact angle above 150 degrees.

## 10. Mosaic optical Fourier surfaces that mix red, green and blue at a 5-millimeter distance

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.21 · Dongjae Baek, KU-KIST Graduate School of Converging Science and Technology, Korea University, and 4 others*

Mixing separated red, green and blue sources over a short distance is a hard problem in compact display optics. Widening the distance mixes them but makes the optics thicker.

Using adjoint design, the team made mosaic optical Fourier surfaces that mix red, green and blue evenly at a viewing distance of 5 millimeters. A 3-millimeter-square aperture holds 225 sinusoidal tiles, with the period, orientation and depth of each tile optimized separately.

The verification is thorough. After designing with a differentiable finite-source model, 102,756 local conditions were calculated directly with vector rigorous coupled-wave analysis. For the 30 highest-risk tiles, 13,766 kernels were replaced with ultra-fine analysis and independent time-domain calculation to check that the results held.

## 11. Radiation patterns reshaped by engraving a metasurface onto the emitter surface

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.24 · Junhyeok Jang, Inter-university Semiconductor Research Center and Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Seoul National University, and 6 others · with Samsung Display*

The performance of light-emitting devices is held back by internal reflection and low light extraction efficiency. What immersive displays demand is extracting more photons and managing the direction light travels.

This is joint research between Seoul National University and Samsung Display.

This paper structures the emitter surface itself in relief and puts a metasurface on it, narrowing the outgoing light and steering it in the desired direction. Radiation pattern adjustments such as deflection and splitting were shown experimentally, and the team also confirmed that no large spectral shift occurs in the process.

## 12. A deep-ultraviolet emitter that sets polarization by electrode shape

*Laser & Photonics Reviews · 10.20 · Yang Kang, University of Science and Technology of China, and 10 others · with Wuhan University*

Precisely controlling polarization in the deep ultraviolet below 280 nanometers has been difficult. The usual approach was to attach bulky external optics, complex metasurfaces or plasmonic structures.

The team changed only the geometry of the p-type electrode. Going from a circle to a microring to a narrow ring, the ratio of transverse magnetic to transverse electric components split into 0.36, 0.54 and 0.77. The −3-decibel bandwidths of the same configurations are 261, 298 and 300 megahertz.

It is not directly tied to displays, but the approach of handling polarization through electrode design without external optics catches the eye.

## 13. Bulk and sidewall in red micro-LEDs separated at cryogenic temperatures

*ACS Photonics · 10.24 · Ming-June Wu, National Taiwan University, and 6 others*

As micro-LEDs shrink, the perimeter-to-area ratio grows and nonradiative recombination at the sidewalls comes to dominate efficiency. Separating and measuring that sidewall component and the bulk component is the purpose of this paper.

Measurements were taken while lowering the temperature from 300 kelvin to 80 kelvin. As temperature drops, the driving voltage rises, yet the external quantum efficiency rises along with it. A modified ABC model including carrier transport and leakage was fitted to size-dependent recombination coefficients, and the surface recombination velocity was extracted from it: 1.53×10⁵ centimeters per second at 300 kelvin and 2.62×10⁴ centimeters per second at 80 kelvin. The effective trap offset of bulk defects came out at 6.05 millielectronvolts.

This one was put up as a candidate for an in-depth article in this issue. Because the micro-LED axis overlapped with earlier issues, it was moved to the briefing.

## 14. An IGZO memristor with oxygen vacancies tuned by nitrogen annealing

*ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces · 10.21 · Yu-Bin Kim, Chungnam National University, and 4 others*

Amorphous indium gallium zinc oxide memristors draw attention on the neuromorphic side for their uniform structure, stable amorphous phase and CMOS compatibility. Resistance switching is mediated by oxygen vacancies, and if their concentration is not controlled, charge transport becomes unstable and switching wavers.

After deposition, annealing in nitrogen from 300 degrees to 600 degrees tuned the vacancy-related defect states. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction showed that the oxygen-deficient component decreased while the amorphous state was maintained.

It is one more case of a display backplane material moving toward memory. It belongs to the same lineage as the IGZO vertical memory covered in issue 1.

## 15. Quantum-dot photodiodes that catch up with InGaAs by covering the non-polar facets

*Nature Communications · 10.22 · Debranjan Mandal, ICFO, Spain, and 6 others*

Colloidal quantum-dot detectors for the 1 to 2 micrometer short-wave infrared are used in autonomous driving, machine vision and night and bad-weather imaging, and they are cheap and compatible with readout circuits. But high dark current has left their performance behind III-V compound semiconductors.

The authors look for the cause of that gap on the surface. Seeing short-wave infrared requires large quantum dots, and large lead sulfide quantum dots develop non-polar facets, which had no good way of being covered.

Lead-thiolate chemistry covered polar and non-polar facets together. The open-circuit voltage loss and the activation energy of dark current fell to the level of single-crystal semiconductors, with a dark current density of about 34 nanoamperes per square centimeter at −1 volt and a specific detectivity of about 8×10¹² Jones. That is on a par with commercial InGaAs photodiodes.

## 16. Cellulose photonic crystal films tuned by salt concentration and ultrasound

*ACS Nano · 10.24 · Diogo V. Saraiva, Utrecht University, the Netherlands, and 8 others · with ETH Zurich*

Cellulose nanocrystals form cholesteric structures on their own and become sustainable photonic crystal films. But their pitch, domain shape and angle-dependent color response have been hard to control.

Varying salt concentration and ultrasound intensity together controlled evaporation-induced self-assembly. 24 films cast in dishes were examined with polarized optical microscopy, time-lapse imaging, rheology, electron microscopy and angle-resolved spectroscopy.

Adding salt shifts the photonic response to shorter wavelengths, while ultrasound breaks up crystal bundles and sends it to longer wavelengths. At higher ion concentrations the ultrasound effect weakens. Applying weak ultrasound at intermediate ion concentration produces wide cholesteric domains and narrows the spectrum.

## 17. Circularly polarized luminescence switched on by twisting lamellae

*Advanced Optical Materials · 10.21 · Yi-Ting Lin, National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, Taiwan, and 4 others · with Osaka Institute of Technology, Japan*

Using spiropyran-attached polylactides, the team looked at how hierarchical chiral structures control photoisomerization, fluorescence and circularly polarized luminescence. Two enantiomeric polylactides provide a crystallizable chiral scaffold, and spiropyran comes in as a light-responsive probe.

Samples made by isothermal annealing were densely crystalline, which locked up the switching between spiropyran and merocyanine; fluorescence was weak and no circularly polarized luminescence appeared. By contrast, annealing with dichloromethane solvent to form banded spherulites let photoisomerization occur even after crystallization, and the two enantiomers produced distinct circularly polarized luminescence signals of opposite sign.

The result is that even with the same material, which annealing builds which higher-order structure switches the optical function on and off.

## 18. A naked-eye VR light field with a 90-degree horizontal field of view

*Optics Express · 10.22 · Jinhong He and 9 others · with Baihe Optoelectronics*

This is a VR three-dimensional light field display viewed without glasses. 3 ultra-short-throw projectors, a directional diffuser and a lenticular lens array create three object-space light fields, and a beam splitter and 3 edge-stitched freeform mirrors magnify and join them in image space.

It produced a continuous depth range at a 90-degree horizontal field of view while keeping resolution. A voxel-based object-image correspondence model was built separately for each channel, working backward from the target image-space light field to the object-space light field each projector must produce.

This one was a candidate last issue but could not be covered because it was in accepted status without an abstract. It was formally published in this window and came in.

## 19. What the collection process caught

This window had 14 candidates with empty abstracts. They split three ways, each for a different reason.

2 cover items. The titles had issue numbers attached at the end. They were a micro-LED dielectrophoretic transfer paper and a dual-gate IGZO laser annealing paper, both Korean research and directly tied to displays, so they came up as candidates. Looking up the original papers showed that **they had already been published on July 23 and July 30 respectively.** Appearing on a cover registered those items with an October date, making them look like this week's candidates.

3 in accepted status. These are Optics Express papers, with page, volume and issue all empty. It is not an access problem; they have not been published yet. Just as item 18 above was in the same state as a candidate last issue and has now been formally published, these will be covered in the next issue once published.

1 correction notice, and the rest were cases where the index did not carry an abstract. Item 1, the electrochromic paper, was one of them. The index had no abstract, but the paper page did.

Treating "no abstract" as one thing means either missing material or putting an old paper into this issue. The markers that separate the three branches are, respectively, the issue number in the title, the empty page, volume and issue fields, and the paper page.
