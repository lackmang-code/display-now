Among display industry news from November 2 to 8, the item that stood out on the technology side was a report that Apple is weighing how to make the oxide TFTs for its next-generation OLEDs. This piece takes that report as a lead, separates confirmed figures from what is only reported, and goes one level deeper.

## News the Press Covered, November 2 to 8

First, coverage of Apple's IT OLED lineup continued. On November 6, ZDNet Korea, citing multiple industry sources, reported that OLED iPads will come in three lines (Pro, mini, and Air) and that Samsung Display will supply the mini. Its shipment forecasts by model were 3 million to 4 million units for the Pro and 4 million to 5 million each for the mini and Air. The article contains no official Apple announcement.

Second, a forecast for display equipment investment came out. In a post on November 4, Counterpoint Research projected that global display equipment investment will fall 49 percent year on year to $5.5 billion in 2027. It put 2026 at $10.8 billion. As the places this year's investment went, it named IT OLED lines such as BOE B16 (written as Gen 8.7 in the post) and Tianma's Gen 8.6 LCD line, and it expects investment to drop sharply next year because there is no large project like BOE B16 Phase 2. The post also says Samsung Display and LG Display are postponing or reducing investment in new technology for Gen 6 smartphone panels.

Third, Samsung Display is running a certified-location program for QD-OLED monitors in China. According to The Elec, Samsung Display said on November 5 that it is running a promotion with six brands including ASUS aimed at Chinese internet cafes. Venues that have installed 30 or more monitors qualify for certification. By the end of October 10 locations had been set up, and the plan is to reach 60 by year-end.

Fourth, two separate stories emerged around Samsung's OLED supply. OLED-Info reported on November 2 that Li Auto said it had abruptly received notice of a temporary supply interruption for the 29-inch 6K automotive OLED that Samsung custom-builds. Li Auto said it will ship part of its output from November 10 to December 17 with LCDs, then swap in OLEDs free of charge starting March 1, 2027. Li Auto did not give a cause. On November 4, citing Taiwanese market reports, the same outlet reported that Samsung had asked UMC for 10,000 or more additional driver IC wafers and been turned down, and the article noted that neither company has confirmed it. Neither source says whether the two stories share a cause. The 22nm driver IC bottleneck was covered in the September 22 issue, so it is not reopened here.

The lead issue for November 2 to 8 is the HMO deposition method for Apple's next-generation OLED.

## What The Elec Reported Is a Weighing of Deposition Methods

In an article posted at 6:43 a.m. on November 3, The Elec reported that Apple, in developing high-mobility oxide (HMO) technology for next-generation OLED panels, is looking at sputtering first as the deposition method. The final mass-production process has reportedly not been decided. The article also says Samsung Display and LG Display are developing both sputtering and atomic layer deposition (ALD).

Here is what the article lays out.

| Item | What the article says |
|---|---|
| Where HMO fits | Named as a candidate technology for high-resolution IT OLEDs such as MacBook and iMac |
| Target | Mobility of 30 to 50 cm²/Vs, versus around 10 cm²/Vs for existing oxide TFTs |
| Samsung Display | Developing sputtering-based HMO and also validating ALD. Apple is said to have proposed that it confirm whether Gen 8-class large-area processing is feasible |
| LG Display | Developing sputtering-based HMO on a Gen 6 small-to-medium OLED line, with ALD in parallel. Leaning toward existing sputtering equipment because of the burden of additional capital spending |
| How the two methods differ | Sputtering can use existing equipment; ALD is more precise, but its equipment and precursor costs are high |

The article rests on statements from industry sources. The text we checked quotes no official announcement or statement from Apple, Samsung Display, or LG Display. So in this piece, the sentence "Apple is looking at sputtering first" is used only as the content of The Elec's report, not as fact.

This is not the first time the topic has come up. On June 2, iClarified, citing The Elec, reported that Apple is evaluating HMO as a possible successor to LTPO. That report said LG Display was developing and validating it by sputtering on a Gen 6 OLED line, and added an industry observation that Apple Watch OLED could be the first application. The Elec's November 3 report is a follow-up that widens the scope to the deposition method and to Samsung Display. MacRumors, citing display analyst Ross Young, said this technology is already used in the new touch MacBook Pro, but the same article lists MacBook and iMac as future applications, and we could not confirm whether the two statements are consistent. We found no evidence that HMO is a name Apple itself uses. It appears to be an abbreviation used by trade media and the industry, but we could not confirm who coined it.

## Raising Mobility Shrinks the Area Needed for the Same Current

What HMO changes follows from what a TFT does. The luminance of an OLED pixel is set by the current the driving TFT delivers. The higher the mobility, the smaller the channel needed to deliver the same current. If mobility rises 3 to 5 times, there is room to make the driving TFT correspondingly smaller. This is our own interpretation from the basic TFT relationship, not something the article says.

Oxide TFTs themselves are not new. According to The Elec's 2022 report, LG Display had been using oxide TFTs in large white OLED for nearly 10 years, and Samsung Display was also using them in Gen 8 QD-OLED. The Elec, however, put the mobility of existing oxide TFTs at around 10 cm²/Vs. What HMO aims for is 3 to 5 times that, 30 to 50 cm²/Vs, and its intended home is the high-resolution IT panels that now use LTPS or LTPO. The article does not explain why large panels get by with a mobility of around 10 while IT panels fall short. Our guess is that the difference lies in pixel size and drive speed.

Today's premium OLEDs use LTPO, a structure that combines LTPS (low-temperature polysilicon) TFTs and oxide TFTs on one backplane. The Elec wrote that if HMO performance is sufficient, some processes could be switched to oxide, reducing process steps and cost. The article's phrase is "some silicon processes," which we read from context as referring to the LTPS side. Apple's products are already split. ZDNet Korea wrote that the iPad Pro uses LTPO and the mini and Air use LTPS, though this is the reporter's own statement with no source given. For the MacBook Pro announced on October 28, Apple likewise disclosed only that it is a tandem OLED with touch support. TFT structure and panel supplier are not in the announcement.

## The 40 cm²/Vs Figure Appears in an LG Display Paper

The Elec wrote that LG Display unveiled a prototype last year achieving about 40 cm²/Vs with an oxide TFT made by ALD, but gave no source. There is a public document whose figure and timing match: the invited paper "Advanced Oxide TFT Technology for OLED Display by Applying ALD Process" by LG Display researchers in SID 2025 Digest 70-2 (pp. 950-953). The article does not say whether The Elec meant this paper, so we do not assert that they are the same.

The paper shows three things.

First, mobility can be selected through composition. Under the same gate insulator and buffer conditions, raising the indium content of the ALD channel to 35, 45, and 84 atomic percent raised mobility to 7.8, 19.7, and 50.4 cm²/Vs. The reference, sputtered IGZO (indium, gallium, and zinc at 1:1:1), is about 10 cm²/Vs. The paper cites as an advantage that ALD can set composition precisely by controlling the amounts of indium, gallium, and zinc precursor separately.

Second, the optimized ALD channel device exceeds 40 cm²/Vs while also improving reliability. According to the paper, under positive bias temperature stress (gate at 30 volts, 60 degrees, 11 hours), threshold voltage shift fell to about 15 percent of that of the sputtered device. The paper attributes this to the ALD film's density of 7.0 g/cm³, higher than the 6.4 g/cm³ of the sputtered film. The paper claims to have improved mobility and reliability together.

Third, there is a demonstration panel. An LTPO AMOLED panel of 6.58 inches, 1200×2640 (441 ppi), driven at 1 to 120 Hz, applies both the ALD interfacial gate insulator and the ALD oxide channel.

What the paper leaves out is also clear. It gives no reliability figures for the 50.4 cm²/Vs device; the mobility curves (Figure 8) show only initial characteristics. Yield, cost, and equipment throughput are also absent. The conclusion, too, goes no further than saying the ALD process could be one of the candidates for AMOLED.

## The Paper Uses ALD in Two Places and Keeps the Time-Consuming Insulator Thin

The paper contains one sentence addressing ALD's weakness. For the gate insulator it uses a double structure, depositing only a few nanometers of silicon oxide by ALD and then building more than 100 nanometers on top by PECVD, and it explains this as a way to cut total process time given ALD's low deposition rate. This interfacial ALD alone improved threshold voltage shift by about 24 percent and reduced the film's hydrogen content by about 43 percent.

ALD appears in two places in the paper. At the insulator interface it is used for only a few nanometers, with PECVD on top to save time, while the channel is deposited by ALD at a thickness of tens of nanometers. The demonstration panel uses ALD in both places. That the channel takes more time is our own interpretation from the paper's statements on thickness; the paper gives no throughput figures.

Separate research aims to solve large-area throughput. In 2016 the Netherlands' Holst Centre said spatial ALD is 10 to 100 times faster and makes a 50-nanometer layer within one minute. This, however, is the research institute's own announcement, and it does not state what the 10 to 100 times is compared against. A paper by TNO and others at SID 2019 deposited IGZO by plasma spatial ALD on substrates up to 32×35 centimeters. What The Elec is talking about is Gen 8-class large area. We could not find public material that bridges the two.

## LG Display Was Ahead in Large-Area Oxide Mass Production

The Elec's October 2022 report said LG Display had been using oxide TFTs for nearly 10 years in large white OLED for TVs and monitors. According to the same article, Samsung Display bought 42 US oxide TFT patents from Fujifilm in September 2022 and was using oxide TFTs in Gen 8 QD-OLED, and its oxide TFT competitiveness for large panels was assessed as a weakness. In other words, large-area oxide is a field LG Display had been first to mass-produce. That report is four years old and says nothing about the situation today.

This latest Elec article says Samsung Display is developing sputtering-based HMO while also validating ALD. One passage stands out: Apple is said to have proposed that the company confirm whether this works at Gen 8-class large area. With Gen 8.6 IT OLED lines going up one after another, one way to read this passage is that large-area uniformity could become the test. Counterpoint expects equipment investment to fall 49 percent next year with no large new projects. That existing sputtering equipment suits a year of shrinking investment better than ALD, which requires new deposition tools, is again our own interpretation. It points the same way as The Elec's statement that LG Display leans toward sputtering because of the burden of additional capital spending.

## What We Looked For and Could Not Find

- Any official announcement or statement on HMO from Apple, Samsung Display, or LG Display
- A granted patent in the name of Samsung Display or Apple that explicitly specifies HMO. There are many oxide TFT patents, but within the scope of this search we could not confirm one that identifies composition and deposition method as HMO
- Public material showing an ALD oxide channel deposited on large-area substrates at mass-production speed
- Long-term reliability figures for a device at the 84 atomic percent indium level

## Outlook

We judge it likely that sputtering will come first as the initial mass-production process for HMO. There are two grounds. In The Elec's article, industry sources said both companies prioritize sputtering and test ALD less because of cost, and LG Display is reported to lean toward existing equipment because of the burden of additional capital spending. The sharply lower equipment investment forecast for next year points the same way.

There are signals the other way as well. LG Display has published a paper with an ALD channel above 40 cm²/Vs and a demonstration panel with improved reliability. The higher the mobility target goes, the stronger the reason to move to ALD. So we do not read this as sputtering winning, only as a possible split between a first product where cost comes first and a later product where performance comes first.

To tell whether this judgment is wrong, watch three things. First, whether ALD oxide deposition tools appear in the two companies' Gen 6 and Gen 8.6 equipment orders. Second, whether large-area uniformity or throughput of ALD oxide channels is disclosed in papers at the next conferences (SID, IMID). Third, whether oxide TFT structure is mentioned in the description of Apple's next IT OLED product. If any one of these is confirmed, the judgment has to be revised.
