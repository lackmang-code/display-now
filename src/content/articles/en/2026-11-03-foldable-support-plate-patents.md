## 1. Part two of the series: the plate behind the screen

[Part 1](/en/article/2026-10-27-foldable-waterdrop-hinge-patents) looked at the shape a screen takes when it folds. The crease starts with the bending radius, and the water-drop hinge decoupled the radius from the closed gap. This part looks at the plate attached directly behind that screen: the support plate. It is the "crease and support plate" cell on the series map.

A foldable screen is an OLED built on a thin film. On its own it cannot withstand even a finger press. So a plate is attached behind it, one that must hold the screen flat when unfolded and bend along with the screen when folded. These two requirements conflict. If it is stiff it does not bend, and if it bends easily it does not support.

Why not make the whole plate solid metal? The support plate given as an embodiment in Samsung Display's February 2020 filing (KR102356652B1) is 150 μm thick stainless steel (SUS 304). The outer surface of a plate bent to radius R stretches by roughly the ratio of thickness to twice the radius (t / 2R). Stainless steel yields once this value exceeds about 0.1~0.5 %, keeping its bent shape even when straightened. Simply bending a 30 μm plate to a radius of 3 mm gives 0.5 %, and a 150 μm plate gives 2.5 %. If the folding band is solid metal, the plate memorizes the crease before the screen does.

The answer the patents found was to cut holes only in the folding band of the plate. The non-folding parts stay solid, and in the folding band elongated holes are cut in a staggered pattern along the fold line. The thin bridges left between holes twist and bend so that the whole plate curves smoothly. The bridges are thin. In the embodiment of the 2020 filing above, the holes are 5.35 mm long and 0.15 mm wide, the bridges between holes are 0.10 mm, and the bridges at hole ends are 0.20 mm. **The holes are wider than the remaining metal.** About thirty-six rows of holes fit in a lattice band 8.65~9.65 mm wide. Samsung Display's 2019 filing (KR102671010B1) described the effect as the hole shape allowing the support member to deform in the length direction rather than the depth direction (paraphrased). In other words, it takes up the fold by stretching sideways rather than letting the screen sag downward.

What this part follows is those holes. In one line: **the holes solved the problem of making the support plate bend, but most support plate patents since then were spent solving problems the holes newly created.**

## 2. The problem written in the filing that first cut the holes (2015)

### 2.1 LG Display, June 2015

In this search, the earliest filing to claim a support plate with holes in the folding band is LG Display's June 2015 filing (KR20160144912A). Claim 1 is simple: a back plate with two or more spaced-apart opening patterns in the folding area. It is two months earlier than LG Display's slit support frame (KR102366299B1) seen in Part 1.

What deserves attention is that the next problem is already written in the same specification.

> "A display defect may occur in which the step caused by the opening pattern (182) is visible on the display surface, and the first and second step compensation layers (186, 188) can prevent such a display defect."

In patent documents, "visible" means seen by the eye. Cutting holes in the plate creates height differences at their edges, and the thin screen sags minutely along those differences. Light reflects differently at those spots, so the hole pattern shows through the screen as lines. The filing that first cut the holes also wrote down a layer to fill them.

### 2.2 Ways to hide them followed

Later filings covered the same problem in other ways. LG Display's 2017 filing (KR102662279B1) inverted the idea. Because only the folding part has holes, the boundary is visible. So sparser holes are cut in the non-folding part as well, to reduce the difference. The specification states the purpose: "it can prevent the pattern deviation between the non-folding part and the folding part from being visible when unfolded."

Samsung Display's 2019 filing (KR102770187B1) described inserting a light-shielding pattern between the plate and the screen to "prevent the spacing (folding area (FR)) from being visible from outside" between support members. The same kind of sentence appears in a 2023 filing (KR20240170626A): "The lower anti-visibility member (TPU) can prevent the lattice pattern of the panel support member (700) from being visible from outside." Eight years on, a separate member is still used to stop the lattice from showing through.

Show-through also put an upper limit on hole size. Samsung Display's October 2021 filing (KR102893631B1) set the hole width at 0.05~0.2 mm and gave reasons on both sides. If narrow, precision machining time increases; if wide, "when the second width (WT2) exceeds 0.2 mm, a problem may occur in which the lattice pattern formed by the plurality of openings (OP) is visible from outside." The bridges between holes are 0.05~0.3 mm. Thinner than 0.05 mm, the bridges may be damaged and holes may join; thicker than 0.3 mm, folding performance drops and stress in the bridges may cause cracks (paraphrased). To bend, the holes must grow; to hide, they must shrink. The narrow window between is today's lattice.

## 3. The second and third problems the holes created

### 3.1 Debris entering the holes

A hole is empty space. During folding and unfolding, dust gets in. Samsung Display's 2019 filing (KR102671010B1) stated the problem: "When foreign matter penetrates the openings, defects of the flexible display device are caused in the folded portion." The solution was to cover the underside of the holes with an elastic member, leaving the holes in place but sealing them from the outside. The February 2020 filing (KR102356652B1) likewise attached a sub cover layer of thermoplastic polyurethane or rubber under the plate, stating that this layer "can prevent foreign matter from entering the first holes (H1)."

### 3.2 When the pen arrived, the metal plate got in the way

The third problem came from outside the screen: the pen. Samsung's S Pen reads pen position using a magnetic field generated by the digitizer under the screen. The digitizer sits below the support plate. If the support plate is metal, the field is blocked by the plate.

In this search, the first to put this problem into a claim is Samsung Display's August 2020 filing (KR20220023952A). Claim 1 defines the two flat-side portions of the support layer as portions "having insulating properties", with a digitizer matched to the two portions beneath them. The specification divides the materials as follows.

> "The first support portion and the second support portion include glass, glass fiber reinforced plastic or plastic, and the folding portion includes metal."

Here the shape of the digitizer in Figure 1 becomes meaningful. The digitizer is split into two halves, leaving the fold line empty. With no coils under the folding band, that part can stay metal. **Metal was left only in the folding band, where there are no coils, and the area above the coils was switched to non-metal.** The same filing also mentioned show-through. It can "prevent the defect in which the loop coils" of the digitizer under the support layer "are visible from above the display panel." This time it was not the plate's holes but the coil pattern below that could show through.

### 3.3 Glass, carbon fiber and then aramid

In the following year or so, the materials changed quickly. A January 2021 filing (KR20220108846A) claimed a glass or ceramic support plate. The specification states that the plate "may be located on the transmission path of the magnetic field or electromagnetic signal", and that a plate with a flat front filters out the steps of the digitizer's conductive pattern so they do not carry up to the screen.

A June 2021 filing (KR103006820B1) stated the condition for the support layer in one sentence.

> "The support layer (PLT) may be selected from materials that can transmit the magnetic field generated by the digitizer (DTM), described later, without loss or with minimal loss."

The material given as an example was carbon fiber reinforced plastic (CFRP). A filing on the same day (KR20220167844A) stated that a reinforced fiber composite support layer may not affect the magnetic field of the digitizer below, and that the holes are made by laser cutting. Four months later, however, an October 2021 filing (KR102893631B1) put aramid fiber into claim 1. Carbon fiber is electrically conductive. The specification states that using aramid raises digitizer sensitivity and antenna efficiency and improves reliability "because generation of conductive foreign matter is prevented." The material changed to solve the pen problem brought back the debris problem, which was again solved with a material.

On the LG Display side, a December 2020 filing (KR20220085201A) claimed a structure in which grooves on the underside of the plate are filled with a filler containing metal fibers. That goes in the direction of filling the holes entirely.

### 3.4 Carbon fiber chooses a direction

A carbon fiber support plate faces one more question than a metal plate: which direction the fibers lie in. Carbon fiber is very stiff along the fiber direction, but across the fibers, the resin binding them carries the load. The same plate may bend easily or resist bending depending on fiber direction.

In this search, the first to put the fiber direction of a fiber support plate into a claim is Samsung Display's January 2021 filing (KR103005271B1). The claim says only "reinforcing fibers"; carbon fiber appears in the specification. It is six days earlier than the glass support plate filing (January 27, 2021). Claim 1 of this filing claims not a material but a direction: the support plate includes a sub support plate "arranged so that the long axis of each of the plurality of reinforcing fibers is parallel to the one direction." Here "the one direction" is the direction in which the folding axis extends. The specification also describes stacking the plate in multiple plies. The top and bottom plies have fibers parallel to the folding axis, with plies whose fibers are perpendicular to the folding axis inserted between them, stacked alternately. Holes are cut in the folding part as in a metal plate. The stated aim is "light weight together with excellent flexibility and good mechanical properties."

The reason lies in bending. When a plate is folded, the outer surface stretches the most and the inner surface compresses the most. The neutral plane in the middle keeps its length. The stretching and compressing run across the folding axis. If the fibers in the outer plies lie across the folding axis, the stiff fibers must be stretched exactly where stretching is greatest. If the fibers lie parallel to the folding axis, that deformation is taken by the resin between the fibers. The cross plies are placed in the middle, where deformation is small, to provide the plate's stiffness.

In a December 2022 filing (KR20240106353A), LG Display gave the same answer with experimental values. The filing builds the support layer by stacking several sheets of prepreg, with carbon fibers laid in one direction and the resin only half cured, and attaches it in the semi-cured state directly under the plate, eliminating the adhesive application step. The thickness is set at 70~90 μm, because thinner than 70 μm the plate is hard to support and beyond 90 μm it is hard to fold. [Table 1] of the specification folded five pairs of support layers of equal thickness, differing only in the fiber direction of the top and bottom plies, 100,000 times at minus 20 ℃.

The results split. All five with outer-ply fibers across the folding axis failed. Of the five parallel to the folding axis, four passed, and the one that failed was 94 μm, thicker than the claimed range. At a thickness of 88 μm, two support layers with nearly equal Young's moduli of 92 GPa and 95 GPa split into pass and fail on outer fiber direction alone. The specification concludes that when prepreg with fibers perpendicular to the folding axis is "located at the top and bottom of the support layer (130), tensile strength increases but folding may become difficult."

**The two companies reached the same rule nearly two years apart: lay the outer-ply fibers parallel to the folding axis.** Of the 63 Korean filings found this time with "carbon fiber", "support layer", "folding", "display panel" and "opening", 62 were Samsung Display's and 1 was this LG Display filing.

## 4. The trend across 145 filings

### 4.1 Problems left by the holes more than by the crease

One or two citations cannot establish a trend. So the full specifications of 145 Korean filings containing all of "lattice", "pattern", "plate", "folding area" and "display panel" were counted by the same criteria. The applicants are Samsung Display with 136 filings, LG Display with 8 and Samsung Electronics with 1. By priority date, filings rose from 14 in 2020 to 41 in 2022, with 27 in 2023 and 29 in 2024.

Laid out on a timeline, the order becomes visible. The holes that allow bending came in 2015, and ways to hide them continued from 2015 to 2021. The pen branch is concentrated between August 2020 and October 2021. The debris branch appears in 2019~2020 as layers covering the underside of the holes, and again in 2021, after carbon fiber came in, as a change of material. The bending branch also reopens in 2021. When the plate became fiber, fiber direction became a new design variable.

## 5. Matched against the product timeline

The pen came to Samsung's foldables with the Galaxy Z Fold3, announced on August 11, 2021. Samsung stated that the S Pen can be used only on the Fold3's inner screen and that only dedicated pens (S Pen Fold Edition and S Pen Pro) are supported (Samsung Electronics Germany Newsroom, 2021-08-31). Of the four pen-branch filings in this search, two (August 2020 and January 2021) precede that announcement, and two (June and October 2021) fall two months before and two months after it.

Then in July 2025, the Galaxy Z Fold7 dropped S Pen support. According to reports, Samsung removed the inner screen's digitizer layer while achieving an unfolded thickness of 4.2 mm and confirmed that the pen is not supported (Android Authority, 9to5Google). Without the digitizer, the third problem in Figure 7 disappears from that product.

However, what material Samsung actually used in each product's support plate could not be confirmed from public sources. Nor is a patent filing evidence of use in that product. What this part can say extends only to the order of filings. Pen-transparent plates were filed around the time the pen was introduced, and how plate filings change after the pen was removed cannot yet be seen, since many filings are still unpublished.

[The DESK article in the same issue](/en/article/2026-11-03-iphone-duo-line-defect) covers the fold defect on Apple's iPhone Duo. This part does not resolve its cause. It only shows how far the list of problems written in support plate patents extends.

### 6.2 What set them apart

The applicants show a split in sequence. The idea of cutting holes and the first ways to hide them were written first by LG Display between 2015 and 2017. From 2019, Samsung Display filled in the cells. The pen-branch filings read in full this time were all Samsung Display's, and Samsung Display accounts for 136 of the 145 lattice support plate filings and 62 of the 63 carbon fiber support layer filings. LG Display reappears in 2022 with carbon fiber ply layup.

This difference overlaps with products. It was Samsung that released a pen-capable foldable in 2021, and when the digitizer went under the support plate, the plate material became a problem. In a foldable without a pen, the third problem never arises. The landscape of support plate patents divided less by who cut the holes first than by who put what under those holes.

## 7. Counterevidence and limitations

The search is a Korean-language full-text search on Google Patents. Because it required both "lattice" and "pattern", filings using only slits or holes may have been left out of the 145. Support plate filings by Chinese and Japanese companies are not included in this count. "Earliest filing" means the earliest found in this search, not that no earlier filing exists.

The 63 carbon fiber support layer filings are Korean filings containing all of "carbon fiber", "support layer", "folding", "display panel" and "opening", and applicants were counted from the patent pages. The folding test values for fiber direction come from the table of a single LG Display filing; the Samsung Display filing claimed the direction but did not give test values in the same format.

The count of 145 was done by sentence pattern. That a specification describes a problem differs from the filing solving it. Only 10 of the 47 show-through filings were checked by eye.

The principle by which the support plate bends is described only to the level of bridges between holes twisting and bending. Dimensions from only two filings (the KR102356652B1 embodiment and the KR102893631B1 range) were carried into the figures. More of the 145 specifications give hole and bridge dimensions, but they differ in plate material and thickness, so they were not combined in one graph, and the relationship between hole size and folding force was not calculated in this part. Filings with numerical values in claim 1 were a minority of the 145 (for example, a fiber layer support plate thickness of 100~300 μm, KR20240007970A) and could not be grouped by the same criteria.

## 8. Conclusion and next part

The plate behind a folding screen had to be stiff yet bend. The patents resolved that contradiction by cutting holes only in the folding band. Yet the 2015 filing that first cut the holes noted in the same document that they show through the screen. Since then, support plate patents have flowed toward hiding the holes, keeping out the dust that gets into them and letting the pen signal pass through the perforated plate. Of the 145 filings, 96 described one of those three, while 40 mentioned the crease. When the plate changed from metal to carbon fiber, one more question arose, and Samsung Display and LG Display wrote the same answer: lay the outer-ply fibers parallel to the folding axis.

Overlaying Part 1 and this part reveals one thing. The crease was the first problem of foldables, but each structure introduced to solve it created the next problem. The water-drop hinge called for space inside the body, and the holes in the support plate brought show-through, debris and the pen.

The next part reads the adhesive layers that bond layer to layer, between the screen and the plate. When folding, each layer stretches and compresses by a different amount, and it is the adhesive layers that absorb the difference. The order of parts may change as the series proceeds.
