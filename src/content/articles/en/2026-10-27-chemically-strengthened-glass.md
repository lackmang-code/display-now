The first cell is chemically strengthened glass. The phone, tablet, laptop and e-reader covers that the ASTM
standard names as its scope are this glass, and the glasses in the other cells stand on the same principle.
This episode looks at how ion exchange lets glass hold up, where the cost of that piles up, and how the

## Breakage starts at a surface flaw

Glass breaks from flaws on its surface. When the tip of a flaw is pulled, a crack grows; when it is pressed,
the crack does not grow. So strengthening cover glass is not about making the glass harder. It is about
putting compression into the surface in advance to keep flaw tips pressed.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-27-chemically-strengthened-glass/flaw-states.svg" alt="A schematic of two sheets of glass with the same flaw being bent. In the unstrengthened glass, bending pulls the top face and a crack runs from the flaw tip. The strengthened glass has a compressive layer on its top face, so the flaw tip sits inside compression and the pulling force must cancel the compression first. Beside each sheet is a through-thickness stress graph adding bending stress and residual stress; at the depth of the flaw tip, the unstrengthened sheet is in tension and the strengthened glass is in compression." />
  <figcaption>If the flaw tip sits inside compression, the pulling force has to cancel that compression before the crack can grow.</figcaption>
</figure>

In a 2013 presentation, Corning researchers named sharp contact as the main cause of failure in
ion-exchanged cover glass, explaining that the glass breaks as flaws made by contact penetrate past the depth
of the compressive layer. A 2017 Corning filing described this as the sharp indentations made when a device
falls onto rough surfaces such as asphalt or concrete.

A 2014 Corning filing wrote that flaws made by such impacts can reach about 200 micrometers below the
surface.

Strengthening barely raises hardness. As we saw in the Part 2 HC episode, the nanoindentation hardness of
ion-exchanged glass is about 8.3GPa, lower than quartz. Chemical strengthening is not a technology that stops
scratches. It is a technology that keeps a scratched spot from spreading into a fracture.

## Why strengthen with ions instead of heat?

**Cover glass is too thin to be strengthened with heat.** Building and car glass is strengthened by heating a
sheet and rapidly cooling its outside with air. The outside sets first and the inside shrinks later, leaving
compression on the outside. This works only when a large enough temperature difference develops between
outside and inside.

In a 2014 filing, Corning wrote that thermal tempering is limited to thicknesses of about 3mm or more. The
same filing said the compressive layer of thermally tempered glass reaches about 21% of the total thickness.
It goes deep, but only in thick sheets.

Asahi Glass (now AGC) also wrote in a 2011 filing that sheets thinner than 2mm, like cover glass, do not
develop a temperature difference between outside and inside, which makes a compressive layer hard to form.
Phone cover glass is in the 0.5-0.7mm range.

So the ions are swapped instead. Dip a sodium-containing glass in molten potassium nitrate (KNO₃), and small
sodium ions leave the glass surface while larger potassium ions take their place. Because the swap happens
while the glass stays solid, the surface layer, with large ions wedged into cramped sites, is squeezed.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-27-chemically-strengthened-glass/ion-stuffing.svg" alt="A schematic of ion exchange. On the left is a molten potassium nitrate salt containing potassium ions, and on the right is the glass. Near the glass surface, large potassium ions occupy the sites of small sodium ions, and deeper in, the original sodium ions remain. The graph below shows the fraction of sites taken by potassium falling with depth from the surface, with a note that because the temperature is too low for the network to soften, the crowded sites become compression." />
  <figcaption>Potassium ions enter sodium sites and the surface is squeezed. Corning describes the bath temperature as about 400℃.</figcaption>
</figure>

Corning's 2008 filing gave bath temperatures of typically 380-450℃ and immersion times from 15 minutes to 16
hours. The resulting compression is 200-800MPa at the surface, with a depth of layer (DOL) from 10
micrometers to over 50 micrometers. An AGC filing gave a range of 350-550℃ for 2-20 hours.

Which glass goes into the bath decides the depth. Corning's 2013 presentation compared two sheets 0.7mm
thick. Alkali aluminosilicate reached a surface compression (CS) of 753MPa at a depth of 45 micrometers, and
soda-lime reached 536MPa at 14 micrometers.

The same presentation noted that when the compressive layer is shallow, as with soda-lime, the cracks that
set strength already extend past it. An AGC filing also wrote that the compressive layer of earlier soda-lime
cover glass was about 9 micrometers.

In a 2018 filing, Corning wrote that adding alumina to the silicate network speeds up the interdiffusion of
monovalent cations during ion exchange. This should not be read as a general rule, though.

A 2022 Frontiers in Materials paper, reviewing earlier studies, noted that in the three-component system of
sodium, alumina and silica, adding alumina reduced the effective diffusion coefficient. The direction depends
on what the alumina replaces.

Raising the temperature speeds things up, but something is lost in return. In a 2011 filing, Corning wrote
that glass undergoes stress relaxation during ion exchange, and that lowering the temperature reduces
relaxation but also lowers exchange speed and depth. The compression put in relaxes by itself at high
temperature.

Chinese researchers strengthening ultra-thin aluminosilicate in pure potassium nitrate in 2023 reported that
beyond 400℃ and 2 hours, surface compression and Vickers hardness dropped sharply due to stress relaxation.
The process window is a choice made between depth and surface compression.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-27-chemically-strengthened-glass/process-order.svg" alt="Five steps of the cover glass process, in order: forming the mother sheet (fusion or float), cutting parts from the sheet, CNC grinding and polishing of the edges, molten-salt ion exchange at about 400 degrees, and anti-reflection and anti-fingerprint coating.   <figcaption>Strengthening comes after cutting and finishing. Cut a sheet whose middle is in tension afterward, and that tension escapes.</figcaption>

The order is fixed too. Corning explains that parts are cut from the mother sheet first and then dipped in
the molten salt. As the next section shows, a strengthened sheet has its middle in tension, so cutting it
afterward lets that tension escape through the new cut face.

How the mother sheet is made differs by company. Corning uses a fusion process, overflowing molten glass from
a trough called an "isopipe" so that the two films flowing down either side join at the bottom. Corning
explains that in this method the glass surface touches nothing, neither hands nor anything else, so no flaws
form.

When it launched Dragontrail, AGC said it is made by the float process, which floats the glass on molten tin
to spread it. If the surface is where glass breaks, so are the edges.

Launching Dragontrail Pro in 2016, AGC said that cases of the rounded edge taking the impact directly in a
drop had increased, and named a strengthened rounded edge as a feature.

## Why does squeezing the surface pull the middle?

**To squeeze the surface with no outside force pushing on it, the same amount has to be paid back as tension in
the middle of the thickness.** For a sheet to stay at rest without bending or stretching, compression and
tension across its section must be equal in area. This tension is called central tension (CT).

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-27-chemically-strengthened-glass/stress-balance.svg" alt="Stress distribution in glass 550 micrometers thick. From each surface, 750 megapascals of compression falls linearly to a depth of 30 micrometers, forming two blue triangles, and the middle 490 micrometers is a red band of tension of about 46 megapascals. The tension is drawn five times larger than the compression. If the area of one triangle is A, the area of the band is 2A, which gives CT = CS·DOL / (t − 2·DOL) = 750 × 30 / 490 = 46 megapascals." />
  <figcaption>In the linear approximation, central tension is CT = CS·DOL / (t − 2·DOL) (Corning US8415013B2 Equation 1).</figcaption>
</figure>

Corning's 2008 filing assumed that compression falls linearly from the surface to depth DOL, and wrote this
relation as an equation. Put 750MPa to a depth of 30 micrometers in a 0.55mm sheet, and about 46MPa of
tension forms in the middle. Put the same compression to 40 micrometers, and it becomes 64MPa.

If central tension is too high, the glass bursts when it breaks. The same filing defined this as
frangibility: breaking into small fragments of 1mm or less, cracks branching in multiple directions, or
fragments flying about 5cm or more. In a 2015 paper, Corning refined this test using more than 2,000
specimens.

The paper flawed sheets 0.4, 0.7 and 1.0mm thick, let them break after a delay, and proposed calling a sheet
frangible if it produced five or more fragments. The approach looks only at the tensile energy stored in the
sheet, not at the impact energy.

The threshold depends on thickness too. The same filing said that a relation considered linear for
thicknesses of 2mm or more is not linear in thin sheets, and put forward a new logarithmic threshold covering
the range that does not burst even at tensions above the linear threshold.

| Thickness | Old linear threshold (−15.7·t + 52.5) | New threshold (−38.7·ln t + 48.2) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.75mm | 40.7MPa | 59.3MPa |
| 0.5mm | 44.7MPa | 75.0MPa |

By the new threshold, a 0.5mm sheet can carry central tension up to 75MPa. The threshold rises as glass gets
thinner, but the tension that a given compression creates grows faster, because the remaining thickness
(t − 2·DOL) shrinks.

  <img src="/articles/2026-10-27-chemically-strengthened-glass/thin-squeeze.svg" alt="A graph of two curves across thicknesses of 0.3 to 1.1 millimeters. The central tension created by 750 megapascals of surface compression at a depth of 40 micrometers rises steeply as the sheet gets thinner, while the dashed frangibility threshold rises gently. The two lines meet at about 0.47 millimeters, and below that thickness the threshold is exceeded." />
  <figcaption>Even with the same compressive layer, a thinner sheet crosses the threshold. Calculated with the linear approximation and Corning's threshold formula.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the first half of the question this episode sets out to answer. To survive drops, compression has to
go deeper than the flaws, but the deeper it goes, the larger the central tension, and the thinner the sheet,
the tighter that margin. In the simulation below, if you increase the depth to bring the flaw tip inside
compression, a 0.7mm aluminosilicate sheet crosses the threshold before compression reaches the tip of a 60μm
flaw.

## How is a depth that survives drops obtained?

**Use a lithium-containing glass and mix sodium into the bath, and compression can be carried deep without
raising central tension.** Here the name for depth splits in two. Depth of layer (DOL) is the value a surface
stress meter reads as the depth of the layer that potassium has entered, and depth of compression (DOC) is
the depth at which stress changes from compression to tension and passes through zero.

In sheets with potassium only, the two values are close, but in lithium glass they diverge widely. In a 2014
filing, Corning wrote that the depth of the flaws that caused failure matched DOC better than DOL.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-27-chemically-strengthened-glass/dol-vs-doc.svg" alt="A figure marking two depths on the near-surface stress profile of a lithium glass. DOL, where the surface potassium layer ends, is 8.9 micrometers, and DOC, where low compression continues for a long stretch until stress reaches zero, is 90 micrometers. Below that is tension." />
  <figcaption>In lithium glass, the DOL that a surface stress meter reads and the DOC where compression actually ends are nearly tenfold apart (values from Example 9 of Corning US11472732B2).</figcaption>
</figure>

In lithium glass, sodium enters the small lithium sites and continues deep into the sheet. In a 2015 filing,
Corning wrote that the depth of compression (DOC) obtained this way reaches about 20% of the thickness.

Corning's 2018 filing measured two kinds of 0.5mm sheet side by side. A lithium-free alkali aluminosilicate
dipped at 420℃ for 330 minutes in a bath of potassium nitrate alone had a surface compression of 857MPa, a
depth of 42 micrometers and a central tension of 68.3MPa.

A glass containing 6.24mol% lithium, dipped at 380℃ for 80 minutes in a bath with 15% sodium nitrate mixed
in, had a surface compression of 747MPa, which is actually lower. But its compression reached 90
micrometers, and its central tension was smaller at 58.6MPa. These are the values of the one specimen, out
of three under the same conditions, whose central tension was also measured.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-27-chemically-strengthened-glass/profile-drop.svg" alt="Stress profiles of two kinds of sheet 0.5 millimeters thick and a bar chart of drop heights. The lithium-free glass strengthened with potassium alone has 857 megapascals at the surface, a potassium layer depth of 42 micrometers and a central tension of 68.3 megapascals. The lithium glass strengthened in a mixed sodium and potassium bath bends from 747 megapascals at the surface to 87 megapascals at a depth of 8.9 micrometers, then its compression continues to 90 micrometers, with a central tension of 58.6 megapascals. Drop heights onto 180-grit sandpaper are 28 centimeters for the lithium-free sheet, 53 centimeters for the lithium glass with 9 percent sodium, and 75 centimeters with 15 percent." />
  <figcaption>Compression twice as deep, central tension smaller. Drop height nearly tripled (Corning US11472732B2 Tables 1·2·7·8·9).</figcaption>
</figure>

In the drop test, each sheet was attached to a phone-sized puck and dropped onto 180-grit sandpaper, with the
height raised in 10cm steps until it broke. The lithium-free sheet broke at 28cm, the lithium glass
strengthened in a 9% sodium bath at 53cm, and the one strengthened in a 15% bath (another specimen under the
same conditions) at 75cm.

For 0.7mm sheets as well, 49cm rose to 74-76cm.

The secret is the shape. The lithium glass sheet puts a thin potassium layer in the top 8.9 micrometers to
carry high compression, and below it draws a low compression of a little over 87MPa all the way to 90
micrometers. Spending the area of compression on depth instead of height leaves the middle less pulled.

That said, this table compares cases in which glass composition and bath conditions changed together. Among
the lithium glasses alone, depth of compression changed only slightly, from 84 to 90 micrometers, yet drop
height differed widely, from 53 to 75cm. Depth alone cannot be read as what sets drop height.

Even so, 90 micrometers falls short of the 200-micrometer flaw depth seen earlier. Putting compression deep
does not cover every flaw. It widens the range of flaws whose tips stay pressed, out to deeper ones.

These specimens were strengthened in one step, in a single bath mixing potassium nitrate and sodium nitrate.
There is also a method that splits it into two steps.

Corning's 2014 filing set out conditions in which the first step builds depth in a mixed bath of 5-90%
potassium nitrate and 10-95% sodium nitrate, and the second step presses the surface again by dipping in pure
potassium nitrate for 15 minutes to 8 hours.

In that filing's worked examples, extending the second step from 4 hours to 8 hours lowered both surface
compression and central tension. Here too, stress relaxation takes its share.

In later filings, Corning wrote that the earlier frangibility criterion holds only when the compressive layer
is much shallower than the thickness. In other words, the safety of a deep profile can no longer be judged by
a single central tension from the linear approximation.

## Gauges for the potassium layer and the sodium tail

**The surface potassium layer and the deep sodium tail are measured with different instruments.** The layer
that potassium enters has a higher refractive index and becomes a thin path that traps light. Surface stress
meters such as the Orihara Industrial FSM-6000 read surface compression and layer depth from the fringe
pattern of the light traveling along this path.

The thin potassium layer at the surface has a raised refractive index and traps light, so the FSM measures surface compression and layer depth. The deep tail beneath it, where sodium replaced lithium, has no raised refractive index and is invisible to the FSM, so the scattered light polariscope SLP measures depth of compression and central tension." />

The tail where sodium replaced lithium does not raise the refractive index, so this method cannot see it. A
Corning filing also wrote that the FSM cannot determine the depth of sodium exchange into lithium sites. So a
scattered light polariscope, which looks at the polarization of light scattered inside the glass, is used.
The Orihara Industrial SLP-2000 measures to a depth of 600 micrometers.

| What | Method · standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Surface compression, potassium layer depth | FSM surface stress meter (guided light) | 0-1,000MPa, depth 5-200μm (FSM-6000X) |
| Deep compression, central tension | Scattered light polariscope (SLP) | Depth 10-600μm (SLP-2000) |
| Grade classification | ASTM C1422 | Classified by surface compression and depth of compression. Does not address end-use performance |
| Surface strength | ASTM C1499 ring-on-ring | Two concentric rings load the sheet in even biaxial tension |
| Drop | Each maker's own sandpaper drop | 180-grit and 80-grit sandpaper, height raised step by step |

With sandpaper grit, a smaller number means rougher. On the Victus 2 product sheet, Corning listed both 1m on
80-grit sandpaper and 2m on 180-grit. The same glass survives half the height when the floor gets rougher,
which is why the floor is the first thing to check when reading a drop height.

What matters is that the standards do not define drops. ASTM C1422 covers chemically strengthened glass for
phones, tablets and laptops, but states itself that its classification criteria are surface compression and
depth of compression and that it does not address end-use performance. ASTM C1279, a photoelastic standard
for thermally tempered glass, explicitly says it cannot be used for chemically strengthened glass.

Drop heights are values each glassmaker measured under its own conditions.

## Glassmakers' competition moved to drop height

**Glassmakers came to compete on drop height onto rough surfaces, and competitors' glass moved to lithium
aluminosilicate.** Corning says it launched Gorilla Glass in September 2007. The 2013 announcement of Gorilla
Glass 3 led with scratch resistance, and the 2014 announcement of Gorilla Glass 4 put drops onto rough
surfaces into numbers. Later generations raised that height.

| Product | Announced | Figure the company claimed (in-house tests) |
|---|---|---|
| Gorilla Glass 4 | 2014-11 | Up to 80% survival when dropped from 1m onto a rough surface |
| Gorilla Glass 5 | 2016-07 | Up to 80% survival when dropped from 1.6m onto a rough surface |
| Gorilla Glass 6 | 2018-07 | Survives an average of 15 drops from 1m. Competing glass broke on the first drop |
| Gorilla Glass Victus | 2020-07 | 2m onto a hard, rough surface. Competing aluminosilicate under 0.8m |
| Gorilla Glass Victus 2 | 2022-11 | 1m onto a surface simulating concrete. Competing glass 0.5m or less |
| Gorilla Glass 7i | 2024-06 | 1m onto a surface simulating asphalt. Competing lithium aluminosilicate 0.5m or less |

These figures cannot be ranked against one another, because the test surface and pass criteria differ by
generation. Corning itself, in a product timeline document, lists Gorilla Glass 5 at 1.2m and Gorilla Glass 6
at 1.6m, which differ from the press releases.

A copy of the Gorilla Glass 3 product sheet listed "50 micrometers at 950MPa," but the sheet Corning now
offers for download only says to ask a sales representative about strengthening conditions.

The supply chain is narrow. Announcing its investment in Corning in 2021, Apple introduced the plant in
Harrodsburg, Kentucky as "where glass for every generation of iPhone has been made." OPPO was announced as the
first customer for Gorilla Glass 7i in 2024.

Competitors moved toward lithium too. SCHOTT introduced its lithium aluminosilicate glass Xensation to Chinese
phone makers in 2011, and in 2021 released Xensation α with added boron.

AGC classifies Dragontrail and Dragontrail Pro as alkali aluminosilicate, and its STAR series as lithium
aluminosilicate.

The same glass is used where screens grow larger. Launching products for automotive interiors in 2019,
Corning said existing cover materials could fail industry reliability tests about one time in three, while
its 2D cover glass was optimized to pass 99% of the time. It also released 3D products bent and attached at
room temperature.

This story returns in the curved and automotive cells of the map.

And in December 2025, AGC announced that it would exit its business in specialty glass for chemical
strengthening. It cited falling prices and weak orders for a business it started in 2009, and production ends
in the third quarter of 2026. One of the main suppliers in this cell is leaving.

## What remains in the strengthened glass cell

The competition in chemically strengthened glass comes down to how deep, and in what shape, compression can
be put in without raising central tension. Reduced to this episode's numbers: by Corning's 2008 threshold,
the central tension a 0.5mm sheet can carry is 75MPa. Lithium glass carried compression to 90 micrometers at
a lower 58.6MPa.

This cell leaves two problems unsolved. One is scratching. Ion exchange barely raises hardness, so the
problem of being scratched by quartz sand remains. The other is the limit of holding up through stress. Once
the compression budget is spent, the next step is to raise the toughness of the glass itself.

Corning's product sheets listing the fracture toughness of Victus at 0.76 and of Victus 2 at 0.82MPa·m^0.5
point in that direction.

The next episode takes that road. Apple introduced the Ceramic Shield of the iPhone 12 in 2020 as raising
drop performance 4 times by including nano-ceramic crystals. Glass-ceramic, which grows crystals inside the
glass, and sapphire, the same crystal as corundum, are the next cells.
