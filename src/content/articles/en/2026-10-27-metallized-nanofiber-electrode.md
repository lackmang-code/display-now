Transparent electrodes carry an unavoidable trade-off. To let light through well, the conductor has to be laid thin or sparse, and then resistance rises. Lay it thick to lower resistance, and light is blocked.

Indium tin oxide held that position for a long time, but its weaknesses in flexible devices are clear. It is brittle, indium is not plentiful, and the process costs money.

So silver nanowires, copper nanowires, conducting polymers and carbon-based materials emerged as alternatives. Their forms branched out too, from thin films to grids, wires and networks.

This paper belongs to the network branch. Nylon is electrospun into a fiber network, and gold or silver is sputtered on top. **It achieved a sheet resistance of 3.05 ohms at 89 percent transmittance.** Raising the transmittance to 95 percent brings it up to 17 ohms. Existing reports in the 90 percent transmittance range mostly sit at 20 to 50 ohms.

## 1. Both processes are mass-production techniques

What deserves attention is the simplicity of the process. Electrospinning and sputtering are both techniques already used in industry, and what the team did was join the two.

They chose nylon 6/6. It has good mechanical strength and chemical stability, and electrospinning can make it into fibers of uniform nanoscale diameter.

The metallization was varied along several lines: metal thickness, network coverage, and whether to coat only one side or both sides. The last variable produces an unexpected result later on.

The finished network can be detached and used as is, or transferred to a flexible substrate such as PET or PDMS.

## 2. Round fibers collect less metal

The diameter distribution shows whether the process ran evenly. Before metallization, the nylon fibers cluster in the 101 to 120 nanometer range, with an average of 112.4 nanometers.

Coating gold at a nominal thickness of 200 nanometers moves the fiber diameter to 251 to 300 nanometers, averaging 289.6 nanometers. Raising it to 300 nanometers moves it again to the 301 to 350 nanometer range, averaging 363.8 nanometers.

One thing stands out here. Measuring the actual gold film thickness by scanning electron microscopy gave 85 nanometers for the 200-nanometer condition and 115 nanometers for the 300-nanometer condition. That is almost half of what would be expected on a flat plate. Because the fibers are round, less metal builds up toward the sides.

The sample coated with 100 nanometers on each side had diameters of 201 to 250 nanometers and the thinnest metal, at 55 nanometers.

## 3. Resistance lowered with almost no loss of transmittance

Optical transmittance was measured over the 400 to 600 nanometer range. Lightly coated samples reach 94 to 95 percent at 550 nanometers, and transmittance falls gradually as the coating gets thicker. Even the most heavily coated network stays above 82 to 85 percent.

Sheet resistance came out at 3.05 to 17 ohms across a transmittance range of 84 to 95 percent. The lowest value is 3.05 ohms, from the sample with 300 nanometers of gold at 89 percent transmittance, and the sample with the highest transmittance, 95 percent, is silver at 16.96 ohms. The best transmittance and the best resistance do not come from the same sample.

The lower bound needs care. The body gives this range as about 3.9 to 17 ohms, and the comparison table gives the gold side as 5.95 to 16.99 ohms, so the lower bounds differ. This article takes the table of measured values as its basis.

Set alongside prior work, the position becomes clear. Existing reports in the 90 percent transmittance range often have sheet resistances of 20 to 50 ohms, and these electrodes are lower than that.

The prior values used for comparison are these. Gold-coated polyacrylonitrile fiber networks gave about 155 ohms at 95 percent transmittance, 25 ohms at 81 percent and 12 ohms at 71 percent. Copper nanofiber networks gave about 50 ohms at about 90 percent transmittance, and metal nanotrough networks at the same transmittance gave 2.8 ohms for copper and 10 ohms for gold and silver. A junction-free silver fiber electrode reported for flexible OLEDs gave 22.3 ohms at 92 percent transmittance.

The nanotrough value of 2.8 ohms is lower than this paper's. So this result should not be read as a record low resistance; its meaning lies in reaching that position with just two steps, electrospinning and sputtering.

Bending was measured as well. After 2,000 bends, sheet resistance was 10.14 ohms for the 200-nanometer gold sample and 3.75 ohms for the 300-nanometer gold sample. The thicker coating also kept its resistance low under repeated bending.

## 4. Splitting the coating across both sides did worse

The value that measures transmittance and resistance together is the figure of merit. Gold-coated samples range from 261 to 808 and silver-coated ones from 246 to 428, and the top value of 808 came from the 300-nanometer gold sample. It means the conductivity gain from a thick, continuous gold film outweighs the loss in transparency.

At the same nominal thickness, gold-coated samples had higher figures of merit than silver-coated ones.

And **the sample coated with 100 nanometers on each side came out with a low figure of merit.** The total amount of metal is the same as coating 200 nanometers on one side, but the result is worse. The authors' interpretation is electrical continuity. Splitting the coating into two thin layers does not create conduction paths as connected as one thick coating does.

It makes sense given the growth behavior of thin films, which form islands and then join up as they thicken. The result says that even with the same amount of metal, where and how it is deposited decides the resistance, and it is a point of reference for anyone coating metal onto flexible substrates.

## 5. A light-emitting device actually switched on

To use the word electrode, current has to flow. The team used the gold nanofiber network as one electrode, connected a red LED bulb in series, and applied 2 volts, the manufacturer's recommended turn-on voltage.

Electroluminescence spectra were measured too. There is a single peak at about 700 nanometers, and the direct-contact case and the case through metallized fibers have the same shape. **It means changing the electrode does not distort the emission spectrum.** Whether color shifts when a transparent electrode is used is the first question the display side asks.

The authors emphasize in their conclusion that the device worked even with the most transparent electrode.

## 6. How far has it come?

The performance numbers are clear. But where this paper stands needs to be seen alongside them.

**The emission demonstration stays at the level of lighting a commercial LED bulb.** The luminance-voltage behavior and emission spectrum with the electrode were measured, but a display electrode faces more tests than that: pixel-level patterning, optical properties after hundreds of bending cycles or more, high-temperature and high-humidity reliability, and the surface roughness that matters when organic layers are deposited on top. Roughness in particular is a point to revisit in this structure. The roughness factor from electrochemical measurement was about 8. In electrochemistry a large effective area is a benefit, but where an organic emissive layer tens of nanometers thick goes on top, it works the other way.

The paper's center of gravity is not displays either. Of the five figures, one is devoted to cyclic voltammetry and electrochemical impedance, and the gap the authors emphasize in comparison with prior work is that "transparent electrospun electrodes have rarely been used in electrochemical systems." To a display reader, it reads as a paper about half filled with sensor material.

It is also worth noting that this is a single-institution study by 4 authors. The simplicity of the process is both the paper's strength and a sign that it has not yet reached device integration.

Still, two things remain. One is a single-digit sheet resistance at 89 percent transmittance, with its position shown against prior values. The other is the result that splitting the same amount of metal across both sides is a loss. The latter, regardless of any performance race, is a finding that can change design for anyone coating conductors onto flexible substrates.
