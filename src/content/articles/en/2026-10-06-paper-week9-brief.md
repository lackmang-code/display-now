From Sunday, September 27 to Sunday, October 4, 2026, papers published in 28 major journals were checked in full and narrowed to **128** with a display connection. Two of those — light extraction in top-emitting OLEDs and leaky-mode metasurface holography — are covered as separate in-depth articles, and **15** are introduced briefly here.

This window was crowded with work on **bringing the screen close to the eye**. A paper organizing the luminance regimes of OLEDoS microdisplays (item 2), a tutorial surveying AR glasses as a whole (item 6), and a paper widening the eyebox of retinal projection (item 7) all arrived the same week.

The other axis is **blue OLED**. An indicator that predicts lifetime non-destructively (item 3) and a molecular design that raises efficiency and color purity together (item 4) came out side by side, and **both are from Korean-affiliated teams**.

Alongside them came three papers on **quantum-dot color conversion films** (items 8 and 9), two on **oxide TFTs** (items 11 and 12), and **electrochromics and electrowetting** (items 14 and 15), which had been absent for a while.

## 1. Larger observer color differences as the price of a wider gamut

*Optics Express · 10.01 · Andrew T. Rider, UCL Institute of Ophthalmology, UK, and 2 others*

Narrowing the primaries to widen the gamut means **the same screen looks different to different people.** CRTs and xenon projectors, which used broadband primaries, had narrower gamuts but looked about the same to everyone.

The team calculated that difference using a model of individual variation in color vision. The displays covered include CRT, PDP, OLED, QD-OLED, PFS LCD, QDEF LCD, LED projectors, RGB lasers and 6P lasers.

The numbers are fairly large. Matching a CRT against a Rec.2020 display requires skewing the red:green ratio by up to **58% and −41%** (by color-vision type), and between a CRT and a pure laser projector it is **83-130%**. Even within normal color vision, the absorption peak of L cones scatters by 3-4 nanometers, starting from a polymorphism carried by **45% of the population**. It should be noted that these are model calculations, not measurements.

## 2. Four OLEDoS regimes divided by sustained rather than peak luminance

*Laser &amp; Photonics Reviews · 09.30 · Rifat Kaçar, Gazi University, Türkiye, and 4 others*

XR devices lose a great deal of light in the optics and combiner. That is why OLEDoS microdisplays have to run **in a range far above the luminance direct-view panels use** for the brightness reaching the eye to be right.

What the team proposes is a classification framework. It divides OLED operation into four luminance regimes, low, medium, high and extreme, with the criterion set by **sustained operation rather than instantaneous peak luminance**.

Put differently, it points out that **the peak luminance on a spec sheet is a number that cannot be used for near-eye displays**. The paper concludes that long-term stability at ultra-high luminance is becoming the key performance criterion for OLEDoS.

## 3. Reading blue phosphorescent OLED lifetime in advance from the magnetic-field response

*Nature Communications · 09.30 · Jeoungmin Ji, School of Electrical Engineering, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), and 7 others · with Kyung Hee University and Hanbat National University*

Measuring lifetime means aging the device. What the paper points out is that conventional lifetime analysis is **accelerated degradation testing that destroys the device**. The same price is paid every time the material changes.

The team showed that the **electroluminescence response under a magnetic field** correlates with lifetime. The magnetic field affects spin-pair dynamics, and those dynamics are sensitive to degradation.

A **linear correlation with over 95% confidence** emerged between the strength of the magneto-EL response (fMEL) and device lifetime, with longer-lasting devices showing lower fMEL. It is a way to gauge stability without burning out the device.

## 4. Bridging resonance to push blue MR-TADF efficiency to 42.6%

*Nature Communications · 09.29 · Yi-Hui He, East China Normal University, and 7 others*

Blue for ultra-high-definition displays needs **narrow emission**, and MR-TADF is a strong candidate for that role. The obstacle has been low efficiency, because exciton dynamics could not keep up.

The team proposed a **bridged resonance structure** that tunes the triplet states. The design raises the photoluminescence quantum yield and narrows the singlet-triplet gap while strengthening spin-orbit coupling.

Device external quantum efficiency was **40.2% and 36.9%**, with color coordinates of CIEy 0.21 for blue and CIEy 0.08 for deep blue. Built as a hyperfluorescent device, it rose to **42.6%**, and efficiency roll-off was also suppressed.

## 5. A review that groups metasurface structural color into "meta-pixels"

*Laser &amp; Photonics Reviews · 10.03 · Jiawei Zhang, Fuzhou University, China, and 9 others*

Metasurface structural color has advantages in resolution and environmental stability, but there had been no review organizing **the conditions displays demand and the outlook at the pixel level**.

This review builds its axis on the **"meta-pixel,"** which bundles spectral control and dynamic tuning into a single unit. Starting from mechanisms such as plasmonic resonance, Mie resonance and mode hybridization, it organizes progress under four headings: color quality, efficiency, angular robustness and ultra-high resolution.

It looks at **the same numbers from a different side** than this week's in-depth article (leaky-mode metasurfaces). This one deals with the pixel, that one with the coherence of the light source.

## 6. A tutorial that surveys AR glasses as a whole in one paper

*Advanced Photonics · 10.01 · Yuge Huang, College of Optics and Photonics, University of Central Florida, USA, and 6 others*

This tutorial explains AR glasses component by component. It starts from perceptual metrics and works down to **light engines** (LCoS microdisplays and emerging micro-LEDs, and how they are driven) and **system architectures** (waveguides and retinal projection).

It also covers auxiliary optics such as beam steering, smart dimming and eye tracking, and brings in AI at the end, for material discovery and optimization of optical components.

**It is a map rather than a paper reporting a finding**, so it is not a target for an in-depth article in this section. It is, however, a good starting point for planners looking into AR for the first time.

## 7. A 10×10 millimeter eyebox widened with a MEMS mirror

*ACS Photonics · 09.30 · Jinlong Xie, Fuzhou University, China, and 9 others*

Retinal projection forms the image directly on the retina, giving a deep depth of field and less eye fatigue, but **the eyebox is small because it is tied to the optical structure.** Existing pupil-expansion techniques widened the eyebox at the cost of optical efficiency.

The team used a **MEMS scanning mirror** to change the angle of a converging laser beam, shifting the exit pupil sideways. The eyebox became **10 millimeters×10 millimeters**.

Optical performance at the central viewpoint is **MTF above 0.3 at 66.7lp/mm**, with system distortion under 3%. Image quality and luminance uniformity across the whole eyebox were confirmed over an accommodation distance of 30-120 centimeters. The point is that it **broke the relationship in which eyebox and efficiency eroded each other**.

## 8. Quantum-dot color conversion layers without a barrier film, two routes

*ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces · 09.28 · Liuqing Han, Hebei University of Technology, China, and 7 others / Advanced Materials Technologies · 09.29 · Aidi Zhang, Nanjing Tech University, China, and 6 others*

The **barrier film** takes a large share of the cost of a quantum-dot color conversion layer. Two papers in the same week each offered a way to remove it.

The first solves it with **surface ligands**. Using two Z-type ligands together to cover anion sites and create a zinc-rich surface, it raised the quantum yield of a quantum-dot photoresist to **87%**. Without barrier encapsulation, after **500 hours of accelerated testing at 85 degrees Celsius** red retained over 90% of its initial emission and green over 83%, and **2-micrometer pixels** were patterned by photolithography.

The second **wraps the particles themselves.** Perovskite quantum dots were encapsulated in silica, made into a powder, sandwiched between optical PET and laminated roll-to-roll. It achieved **96.04%** of the DCI-P3 gamut without using expensive commercial barrier films.

## 9. A color conversion film that kept 60% after 28 days in water

*Small · 09.29 · Seung Ryeol Song, Department of Convergence Display Engineering, Yonsei University, and 6 others*

Perovskite quantum dots have good color purity but are **vulnerable to water.** The team encapsulated them in a copolymer of vinyl chloride grafted with methyl methacrylate (PVC-g-PMMA). The methyl methacrylate side covers defects, and the vinyl chloride side blocks water and chemicals.

Quantum yield rose from 16.32% to **50.64%**. After immersion in water, **over 60% of initial emission remained at 28 days**, while the methyl-methacrylate-only version was under 20% at 7 days. Under 4 hours of ethanol exposure, the split was over 90% versus under 10%.

The team also pinpointed the cause. Counting particles under transmission electron microscopy showed that **particle agglomeration was suppressed**, preserving size uniformity, with the coefficient of variation of the maximum Feret diameter staying at 0.25-0.27. The control rose as high as 0.81.

## 10. Spin QLEDs with a 3,969-hour lifetime and 14,370-nit luminance

*Advanced Science · 09.27 · Fengqi Qiu, Guangdong, China, and 8 others*

The aim of spin LEDs is to emit circularly polarized light directly from the device, without a polarizer and λ/4 plate. The obstacles have been interface quality on the hole transport layer side and transport of spin-polarized charge.

The team **blended** the hole transport layer. PVK has good wettability (contact angle 27.84°) but low mobility, while TFB has a mobility of 10⁻²cm²/V·s but is so hydrophobic (45.15°) that the solution beads up into islands. Mixing them 2:1 brought the contact angle to **39.51°**.

At 100cd/m², the T50 lifetime is **3,968.9 hours**, with a red peak luminance of **14,370cd/m²**, external quantum efficiency of 6.68% and a circular polarization dissymmetry factor of 0.026. However, **the "improvement by several orders of magnitude" is a comparison within the spin-LED field**, and it is still far from the efficiency of ordinary red QLEDs (in the 20% range).

## 11. IGZO mobility and threshold voltage controlled together with a gallium-oxide buried channel

*ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces · 09.28 · Taeyoon Lee, Seoul National University, and 6 others*

IGZO has extremely low off-state leakage, making it a candidate for DRAM cell transistors. The problem is that **as an n-type material its threshold voltage goes negative**, and raising mobility by adding indium or reducing gallium pushes the threshold voltage further negative and worsens bias reliability.

The team designed a **buried-channel stack with gallium oxide on the gate side and IGZO buried beneath it**. They varied the gallium-oxide thickness and the thickness and composition of the IGZO beneath it together, while keeping the total semiconductor thickness nearly constant.

The result eased the relationship in which mobility, threshold voltage and bias reliability eroded one another. **It is a DRAM design, but it reads directly for anyone dealing with the same trade-offs on the backplane side.**

## 12. An indium-free oxide TFT, tungsten-doped tin oxide

*Applied Physics Letters · 09.29 · Mansi Anil Patil, IIT Bombay, India, and 6 others*

Indium supply is a constant risk for oxide semiconductors. The team deposited a **tungsten-doped tin oxide** channel below 10 nanometers thick by atomic layer deposition at 150 degrees Celsius.

The device with 10% tungsten performed best, and a post-fabrication **5-minute oxygen anneal at 300 degrees Celsius** greatly boosted its characteristics. The subthreshold slope was nearly halved, the on/off ratio went from 10⁷ to **10⁹**, and hysteresis shrank to about 1/3.

Threshold-voltage shift under positive bias stress also improved more than twofold, to **93 millivolts at a field of 4MV/cm**. Monte Carlo simulation attributes the instability to charge trapping in the gate insulator and at the interface.

## 13. A phase diagram and a piezoelectric constant for ferroelectric nematics in the same week

*Nature Communications · 10.03 · Aditya Vats, University of Ljubljana, Slovenia, and 2 others / Advanced Science · 09.27 · Péter Salamon, Wigner Research Centre for Physics, Hungary, and 4 others*

Ferroelectric nematics are materials in which **spontaneous polarization** accompanies the orientational order of a liquid crystal. They are not yet panel materials, but theory and measurement appeared side by side in this window.

The theory paper **completed the bulk phase diagram**. Within a Landau-de Gennes free-energy framework, it reproduces the ferroelectric nematic, antiferroelectric splay, nonpolar nematic and isotropic phases **down to the order of phase transitions**, and predicts a new intermediate phase (a periodic splay-bend structure) created by a bending instability.

The measurement paper quantified the **direct piezoelectric effect** for the first time. Until now only the converse effect, applying voltage and measuring deformation, had been measured; the team applied periodic shear to two compounds, RM734 and DIO, measured the current, and ran oscillatory rheology alongside to obtain the **shear-mode direct piezoelectric coupling constant**. It proposes a mechanism in which flow aligns the ferroelectric polarization.

## 14. The less-noticed side of electrochromics: the ion storage layer

*ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces · 09.29 · Seungwon Lee, Chung-Ang University, and 10 others*

Electrochromic device papers usually deal with **the material that changes color**. What jointly sets charge balance, switching speed, optical neutrality and bistability is the opposite **transparent capacitive ion storage layer**, which has received relatively little research.

The team placed nanostructured ITO particles and PEDOT:PSS in that position and compared them. **The ITO particles stably delivered optically neutral double-layer capacitance**, giving a fast response and high coloration efficiency.

**PEDOT:PSS behaved the opposite way.** It showed pseudocapacitive behavior with mixed ionic and electronic flow, and **its optical memory stood out.** Electrochemical analysis showed that ITO keeps its morphology and crystallinity, while PEDOT:PSS undergoes changes. **Whether you get fast switching or memory is decided in this layer.**

## 15. Electrowetting pixels split into four spatial states

*Advanced Functional Materials · 09.28 · Shipeng Wu, Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Optical Information Materials and Technology, China, and 10 others*

This is research on optical encryption using an electrowetting display. What catches the eye from the display side is not the encryption but **the way pixels are handled**.

The team used inkjet-printed conductive micro-pillars to **locally strengthen the electric field at the four corners of a pixel.** The oil film ruptures at a set spot and retreats in one direction, creating **four spatial states**. Cyan, magenta and yellow are then stacked vertically for subtractive color mixing.

Switching time is **25 milliseconds**, and the areal information density converted with a Base64 mapping is 1.77×10⁸ bits per square meter. A feature of this approach is that patterns can be programmed without a mask.
