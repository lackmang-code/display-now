From Monday 14 September to the morning of Sunday 20 September 2026, **1,149** papers published in 28 major journals were checked and narrowed to 117 with a display connection. Two of those, a polariton OLED and a blue upconversion OLED, are covered as separate in-depth articles, and **12** are introduced briefly here.

**Because the issue comes out on a Tuesday, this week's sweep ran only to Sunday morning.** Whatever was published on the afternoon of the 20th will be looked at again in the next issue's window.

What clustered this week was **work that pulls more light out without changing the emitting material**. The polariton OLED covered as an in-depth article is one, and the plasmon recovery in item 2 and the top-emission narrowband design in item 3 work on the same place.

Beside them stood **two papers taking oxide semiconductors outside the display**. Stacked three layers high on a silicon wafer, and with the channel length cut to 10 nanometres, they head into memory.

## 1. A stretchable quantum-dot screen printed at 16,000 pixels per inch

*Nature Nanotechnology · 09.14 · Jisu Yoo, Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology (UNIST), and 25 others*

In stretchable emissive devices, **softness, charge injection and pixel resolution eat into one another.** Put quantum dots into a rubbery substrate and that polymer becomes an insulating barrier, and a soft film is hard to pattern finely.

The team layered two things. They **replaced only the polymer at the surface of the quantum-dot composite film with short polar ligands**, lowering the hole injection barrier while leaving the inside soft. Then, by transfer printing with an intaglio plate under heat, they **cut the soft film cleanly at the pattern boundary.**

A conventional structure gave an external quantum efficiency of 23.9%, and a fully stretchable structure gave a luminance of 53,300cd/m² at an efficiency of 8.0% with stretchability above 65%. Resolution reached **16,000 pixels per inch**, and a 12×12 multicolour passive-matrix screen was built.

## 2. Recovering 30% of the light that leaked into surface plasmons

*Nature Photonics · 09.15 · Thi Thuy Truong, Department of Information Display, Kyung Hee University, jointly with the University of Cologne, Germany*

A large share of the light made inside an OLED never gets out. It is trapped in waveguide modes and in the **surface plasmon polariton modes** of the metal electrode, and vanishes as heat.

The team put a **colloidal quantum-dot interlayer** into an inverted phosphorescent OLED. Time-resolved emission measurements confirmed plasmon coupling between the emissive layer and the quantum-dot layer, and three-dimensional finite-difference time-domain simulation showed that the layer changes the photonic density of states and increases the light escaping towards the air.

The quantum-dot interlayer **intercepted about 30.7%** of the energy headed for plasmons, and external quantum efficiency went from 15.67% **to 20.95%**, a factor of 1.3. The same Humboldt centre at the University of Cologne that appears in the in-depth polariton OLED article took part here too.

## 3. A deep-blue platinum emitter narrowed to a 13.3nm linewidth

*Advanced Functional Materials · 09.15 · Jieying Tong, Research Center for Phosphorescent Materials, Zhejiang University of Technology*

In blue OLEDs, raising efficiency muddies the colour, and purifying the colour drops the efficiency. The index that reads both as a single number is the **blue index (current efficiency divided by the CIE y coordinate)**.

The team **gave the platinum(II) emitter a three-dimensional molecular skeleton** and tuned its excited-state properties along with it. The bottom-emission device gave a full width at half maximum of 19nm at 461nm, an external quantum efficiency of 29.1% at 1,000cd/m² and 22.9% at 10,000cd/m², with a peak luminance of 49,971cd/m².

In the top-emission device the linewidth narrowed to **13.3nm** and CIE y fell to **0.053**, very close to the BT.2020 blue reference. The blue index reached 464 at best and held at 435 even at 1,000cd/m².

## 4. An IGZO nanofibre transistor gated through an electrolyte

*Nano Letters · 09.18 · Shi-Kai Shi, Southeast University, China, and 9 others*

Using an electrolyte in place of a gate insulator lets a thin electric double layer formed by ions control the channel, so the transistor turns on at **a low voltage of around 1V**. Speed and stability have been the catch.

The team electrospun IGZO into **nanofibres** to raise the surface area. Gated with phosphate-buffered saline, the device gave an on/off ratio of about 6.3×10<sup>4</sup>, hysteresis of about 40.4mV, and turn-on and turn-off times of 201 and 347 microseconds.

With an ion-gel electrolyte it withstood bias stress better, allowing **6,000 seconds of continuous switching**. Solid-state devices were tied into an inverter and NOR and NAND gates and **operated at 1V**, and were built on a thin polyimide substrate as well.

## 5. EQE 36.7% by mixing a small molecule into the emissive layer

*Advanced Functional Materials · 09.16 · Jialei Chen, Institute of Optoelectronic Technology, Fuzhou University*

Quantum-dot LEDs suffer from mismatched electron and hole transport that piles charge up in the emissive layer. The team used **Langmuir-Blodgett transfer** to insert the small molecule mCP inside the quantum-dot emissive layer.

At a quantum-dot to small-molecule mass ratio of 10:1 they obtained an external quantum efficiency of **36.7%**, a current efficiency of 58.5cd/A and a power efficiency of 89.3lm/W, with emission at pure red 622nm and a full width at half maximum of 17.71nm.

Lifetime is written as a T95 of **35,064 hours** at 1,000cd/m², more than three times the control, but **that figure is extrapolated, not measured**. Reading it as a multiple is safer.

## 6. An evaporated perovskite LED better passivated by larger-dipole molecules

*ACS Applied Electronic Materials · 09.18 · Yongqian Yang, Soochow University*

Most perovskite LEDs are made by solution processing, but **thermal evaporation** is the convenient route if an existing OLED line is to be used. Evaporated CsPbBr<sub>3</sub> films, though, carry many surface defects and charge injection skews to one side.

The team attached three phenethylammonium-family molecules in turn and compared how well **the size of the molecular dipole moment** passivates defects. The material with the largest dipole bound most strongly to undercoordinated lead sites.

The best device gave an external quantum efficiency of 11.9% and a peak luminance of 40,341cd/m². The usefulness of the paper is that it reduces the criterion for choosing a molecule **to the dipole moment alone**.

## 7. Changing pixel colour by thinning nanowires

*Advanced Optical Materials · 09.17 · Zichun Li, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology*

For AR and VR microdisplays the task is **getting several colours out of a single gallium nitride wafer**. Instead of growing a different composition for each colour, this paper etches down an already grown green epilayer.

Nanowires were formed by inductively coupled plasma etching, and as the **diameter came down from 2 micrometres to 100 nanometres** emission moved 16nm towards the short side. Green 504nm through blue 488nm was tuned continuously on one wafer.

Three-dimensional simulation points to the reason. As the diameter shrinks, **the strain-relaxed shell takes a larger share**, the internal piezoelectric field is suppressed and the energy bands flatten. That the colour range is only 16nm wide is a limitation of the paper itself.

## 8. Oxide semiconductors stacked three layers high on a 200mm wafer

*Nature Nanotechnology · 09.15 · Chang Niu, Purdue University, USA*

Oxide semiconductors are the material of display backplanes, but because they can be made at low temperature they are **well suited to sitting on top of silicon circuits**. The team stacked indium oxide devices made by atomic layer deposition **three layers high on a 200mm silicon wafer**.

More than 100,000 devices were made, with a threshold voltage standard deviation down to 0.04V and an average electron mobility up to 91.6cm²/V·s. Circuits running across the layers worked as well.

A **compute-in-memory accelerator** built four layers high was 1.4 to 2.9 times faster than a two-dimensional baseline on large language model workloads. This is the path by which a material refined in display processing crosses over to artificial intelligence hardware.

## 9. A DRAM built from oxide transistors with a 10nm channel length

*Nano Letters · 09.19 · Weimin Pan, Hunan University, and 16 others*

Oxide semiconductors leak very little current when off, which makes them a candidate for **capacitor-less DRAM (2T0C)**. The problem is shrinking the channel to 10 nanometres, which lithography struggles to do.

The team set the channel length with a **sidewall spacer** formed by letting aluminium oxidise on its own. It is a self-aligned approach that does not lean on lithographic resolution.

In a six-nanometre-thick channel with reduced indium content they obtained a maximum current of 540µA/µm and an on/off ratio of about 10<sup>8</sup>, and the 2T0C DRAM built from it showed a data retention time of **about 80 seconds** at room temperature.

## 10. A cholesteric liquid crystal whose colour keeps changing while the light shines

*ACS Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces · 09.18 · Yixi Liu, Guangdong University of Technology, and 13 others*

In cholesteric liquid crystals the pitch of the helical structure sets the reflected colour. Adding a light-responsive chiral dopant lets the colour be changed, but **the changed colour usually just stops there.**

The team turned around **the way light weakens with depth** in a polymer-dispersed cholesteric liquid crystal. As the degree of photoisomerisation differs between top and bottom, a gradient appears in the helical pitch and the structural colour goes on changing over time.

Tuning how much the light is attenuated sets **the speed at which the colour changes**. That leads on to anti-counterfeiting patterns read by time and temperature together, and to display materials that record thermal history.

## 11. 3D meta-holography stretched to a depth of one metre

*Nature Communications · 09.16 · Di Wang, Beihang University, and 14 others*

Holograms made with metasurfaces have stayed at **a reconstruction depth range of the centimetre order**. Controlling a separate image for each polarisation has been difficult too.

The team set up a polarisation-multiplexed three-dimensional Fourier diffraction principle and attached **a 6mm-diameter polarisation liquid lens** to it. A liquid crystal light valve is included so that focal length and polarisation state change together under a single voltage.

The depth of the reconstructed image reached **the metre scale**, about 80 times that of conventional Fourier meta-holography. The applications the paper names are encryption, optical storage and super-resolution imaging.

## 12. A stretchable TADF polymer that knits back together in 15 minutes after a break

*Advanced Functional Materials · 09.17 · Wenzhong Bian, Jiangsu Ocean University, China, and 11 others*

In stretchable displays self-healing ties directly to lifetime. The team put TADF emitting units, long alkyl chains and **disulphide bonds together with polyurethane hydrogen bonds** into a single polymer backbone.

The film gave a photoluminescence quantum yield of up to 78% and an elongation at break of 65%, and **in 15 minutes at 50℃** its structure and optoelectronic properties recovered. A cyan device gave an external quantum efficiency of 18.56%, and a white device blending in a red dye gave 19.17%.

Used as a multi-resonance TADF sensitiser it also makes a narrowband blue device with a 32nm full width at half maximum (EQE 14.35%). Emission held when bent to a 2mm radius, and after **ten cycles of cutting and rejoining about 80% of the initial performance** remained.
