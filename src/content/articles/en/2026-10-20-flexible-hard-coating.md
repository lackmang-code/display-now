The hard coat at the very top of a foldable screen must not scratch, and it must fold countless
times. As we saw in the previous episode, a hard coat on film is a layer that lends hardness to a soft
base. The easiest way to raise hardness is to apply it thicker.

On film that folds, that road is blocked. **One layer has to carry two demands at once: the harder you
make it, the harder it is to fold.** This episode looks at where that trade-off comes from, how the
industry solved it, and where the hard coat went after cover windows moved to glass.

## Thicker is harder, and thicker cannot fold

**Apply the same hard coat thicker and pencil hardness goes up, while the radius it can be folded
outward to grows larger.** BenQ Materials recorded this trade-off in a single table in a 2019 filing.

The substrate is a 30-micrometer colorless polyimide. The same coating, an acrylate mixed with reactive
silica nanoparticles, was applied at seven thicknesses, and 750g pencil hardness and fold durability
were measured.

| Hard coat thickness | Pencil hardness (750g) | Outward fold pass radius | Inward fold |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2μm | 6H | 1mm | Passes 1mm |
| 3μm | 6H | 2mm | Passes 1mm |
| 6μm | 6H | 2mm | Passes 1mm |
| 13μm | 7H | 3mm | Passes 1mm |
| 14μm | 8H | 3mm | Passes 1mm |
| 17μm | 8H | 4mm | Passes 1mm |
| 25μm | 9H | 5mm | Passes 1mm |

The fold test folds the film 180 degrees and unfolds it 200,000 times, then checks for cracks or
breaks. An outward fold puts the hard coat on the outside; an inward fold puts it on the inside.
Outward fold radii were tested from 1mm to 5mm in 1mm steps.

The coating mixed two kinds of silica into a urethane acrylate oligomer: silica surface-modified with
acrylic groups, and silica carrying silsesquioxane (POSS). It was cured with UV at 298mJ/cm² in
nitrogen.

**The 25 micrometers that earned 9H would not fold outward any tighter than a 5mm radius.** Conversely,
the 2 micrometers that folded outward down to a 1mm radius stayed at 6H. Folded inward, all seven
thicknesses passed a 1mm radius.

The same film differed by up to five times in radius depending on the folding direction. Why it splits
like this only when folded outward is the first question of this episode.

## Why does it break only when folded outward?

**When a film is folded, the inner face shrinks and the outer face stretches.** Between them lies a
plane that neither stretches nor shrinks, called the neutral plane. How much a face stretches or
shrinks, that is the strain, is close to its distance from the neutral plane divided by the radius.

Put the hard coat outside and the coating is stretched. Pull on a hard film that does not stretch
well, and a crack forms across the film and runs sideways. Put it inside and the coating is
compressed. In a compressed film, cracks do not open.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-20-flexible-hard-coating/inside-vs-outside.svg" alt="A figure comparing two films folded around a rod. On the left, the hard coat is on the inside toward the rod and is compressed, so the cracks stay closed. On the right, the hard coat is on the outside and is stretched, so the cracks open. Both drawings show, as a dashed line near the middle of the film thickness, the neutral plane that neither stretches nor shrinks." />
  <figcaption>Figure 2. The same film is compressed or stretched in the coating depending on which side the hard coat is on when folded. Cracks spread on the stretched side. <span class="src">Suo et al. 1999 · drawing by this magazine</span></figcaption>
</figure>

In thin-film fracture mechanics, a compressed film needs a pre-existing lifted spot that has to buckle
up before it starts to separate. A film under tension has no such precondition. In 3M's experiments as
well, the same coating wrapped a 2mm-radius rod in every case on the inside, but needed 3-6mm on the
outside.

Why thickness matters comes from two things overlapping. First, as the coating gets thicker, its outer
face moves farther from the neutral plane and stretches more at the same radius. Second, the limit the
film itself can withstand drops.

**The strain at which cracks begin to spread in a brittle film under tension is inversely proportional
to the square root of the film thickness.** The energy released as a crack advances one step grows
with film thickness, while the film's resistance to stopping the crack does not depend on thickness.
Four times the thickness means half the strain it can withstand.

The substrate plays a part too. In the same theory, the softer the substrate is than the film, the less
the film is held in place and the greater the force driving the crack. A film with a hard hard coat on
soft polyimide is, in that sense, an unfavorable pairing.

We checked the two effects of thickness against BenQ's table. We set the moduli of the polyimide and
the hard coat both to 6GPa and calculated the outer-face strain at each radius. The limit was a single
"constant ÷ √thickness", and taking the constant as 2.7-2.8%·√μm matches the radius in all seven rows.

The same phenomenon appears in nanometer films. In a 2021 study that put a silicon-oxide-based (SiOC)
film on 50-micrometer CPI, after 100,000 folds at a 2mm radius the 100-nanometer film was intact both
ways, while the 300-nanometer film cracked in the outward fold.

Calculated with the formula that study used, the outer-face strain of the two films is nearly the same,
around 1.25%. **What split them at the same radius was not how much they stretched but the limit they
could withstand.** In the simulation below, you can see that boundary by changing the thickness, radius
and folding direction.

## What does a fold radius measure?

**"Passes a 1mm radius," like 9H, is a number that can only be compared when its conditions are
written with it.** Even the tests cited in this episode differ in hard coat direction, whether they
repeat, and how many times.

| Source | Method | Hard coat direction | Cycles | Value recorded |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BenQ | Fold and unfold between two plates | Inside and outside separately | 200,000 | Radius passed |
| 3M | Wrap 180 degrees around a cylindrical rod | Inside and outside separately | 1 | Thinnest rod wrapped without cracking |
| 3M | Fold and unfold between two plates | Outside | Up to 300,000 | Cycles to failure |
| Kolon | Bend while narrowing the radius | Coated face up | 1 | Radius at first crack |
| Toyobo | Fold and unfold, 3mm radius | Inside | 200,000 | Pass or fail |

BenQ's equipment changes the distance between the two plates from 2mm to 10mm to make radii of 1-5mm.
The radius is half the distance between the plates. 3M set the plate gap at about 8mm and folded about
30 times a minute, and its rod-wrapping test adapted the paint standard ASTM D522.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-20-flexible-hard-coating/fold-tests.svg" alt="A figure of three fold test methods. The first is dynamic folding, with both ends of the film attached to two plates that are repeatedly closed and opened, where the distance between the plates is twice the radius. The second wraps the film 180 degrees around cylindrical rods to find the thinnest rod it wraps without cracking. The third is a static test that narrows the radius little by little and measures the radius at which a crack first appears." />
  <figcaption>Figure 4. Even the same "R" means something different depending on whether it is the result of repeated folding or of a single wrap or bend. Which side the hard coat was on also has to be checked. <span class="src">US11326072B2 · US11827811B2 · US11117355B2 · drawing by this magazine</span></figcaption>
</figure>

Kolon's method has no repetition. It bends once and measures the radius at which a crack first
appears. **A radius passed after 200,000 folds and a radius at which a crack appeared after one bend
are different values even when written as the same "R".**

There are examples of repetition changing the result. A coating published in Nature Communications in
2024 withstood 10,000 U-shaped bends at a 1.5mm radius on CPI, but cracked after only 300 at 1.0mm.

The same problem exists on the pencil side. In a pencil test at 1kg load, 3M recorded two values
separately. The grade at which the PET was pressed first and left a dent was mostly 2H, and the grade
at which the hard coat cracked was 4H-6H. It is the previous episode's "the soft substrate sinks along
with it" stamped as numbers.

## Lay a soft layer, put a hard layer on top

**To make it fold without reducing thickness, simply avoid making the whole film hard.** The published
solutions fall broadly into three.

The first is controlling how tightly the particles in the coating are bound to the resin. 3M applied a
5-micrometer hard coat containing 58 wt% of 20-nanometer silica to PET, and compared versions that
changed only the ratio of reactive groups on the particle surface.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-20-flexible-hard-coating/semi-reactive-particles.svg" alt="A comparison table figure of three particle surface treatments. Particles with fully reactive surfaces needed a 6-millimeter radius with the hard coat outside, failed after 500 folds and passed abrasion. Particles mixing reactive and non-reactive groups at 5 to 95 reached 4 millimeters, over 200,000 folds and passed abrasion. Particles with only non-reactive groups reached 3 millimeters and over 200,000 folds but wore away at 1,000 abrasion cycles. With the hard coat inside, all three cases were 2 millimeters." />
  <figcaption>Figure 5. Reducing reactive groups on the particle surface improves folding; removing them all worsens abrasion. Fold cycles are results of repeated folding with the hard coat outside at a plate gap of about 8mm. <span class="src">US11827811B2 Table 7 · drawing by this magazine</span></figcaption>
</figure>

With fully reactive particle surfaces, it did not wear when rubbed, but needed a 6mm radius for an
outward fold and failed at 500 repeated folds. With no reactive groups at all, it folded more than
200,000 times but wore away after 1,000 rubs.

**Particles mixing reactive and non-reactive groups at 5 to 95 delivered a 4mm radius, over 200,000
folds and an abrasion pass together.** In the same table, specimen curl also fell from 5.0mm to 0.5mm
as reactive groups were reduced. Curl, the homework from the previous episode, shrinks along with it.

The second is splitting the layer in two. Kolon Industries filed a structure that lays a soft layer of
Vickers hardness 40 or less on polyimide and puts a thin hard layer of 55 or more on top. Both layers
are siloxane-based.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-20-flexible-hard-coating/soft-hard-two-layer.svg" alt="Cross sections and results of three coatings on polyimide. 10 micrometers of soft layer only did not crack down to a radius of 1.0 millimeter or less but was scratched by steel wool. 10 micrometers of hard layer only cracked at a 3.0-millimeter radius and was not scratched. Stacking 8 micrometers of soft layer and 2 micrometers of hard layer held down to 1.2 millimeters and was not scratched." />
  <figcaption>Figure 6. Splitting the same 10 micrometers into 8 of soft layer and 2 of hard layer handles both scratching and folding. The radius is the value at which a crack first appeared with the hard coat facing up while narrowing the radius. <span class="src">US11117355B2 Tables 2·3 · drawing by this magazine</span></figcaption>
</figure>

A hard layer alone at 10 micrometers withstood steel wool but began to crack at a 3.0mm radius. A
soft layer alone at 10 micrometers bent down to 1.0mm or less but was scratched by steel wool.

**Split into 8 micrometers of soft layer and 2 of hard layer, it held to a 1.2mm radius without being
scratched.** The hard layer is on the outer face, and the soft layer takes most of the thickness. By
the formula in the previous section, the thinner the hard layer, the higher the limit at which cracks
spread.

The fracture strain from tensile testing was also recorded in the table. The hard single layer broke
at 3.0%, and the two-layer structure at 6.3%. The soft single layer stretched to 31% but did not stop
scratches.

In the same patent, Kolon did not use pencil hardness, calling it a test that relies on human visual
judgment, and graded performance by indentation hardness and bending radius. It is the same concern as
the previous episode's "9H can only be compared when the load and substrate are written with it."

The third is making only the folding area different. In a 2015 filing, Samsung Display put forward a
structure with a hard coat of uniform thickness on the top face of a plastic cover window, and a hard
coat on the bottom face that is thinner than its surroundings only in the folding area.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-10-20-flexible-hard-coating/fold-zone-thinning.svg" alt="A schematic cross section of a plastic cover window. The top hard coat is about 35 micrometers and uniform throughout. The bottom hard coat is about 5 micrometers at the periphery and thinner than that in the central folding area. The bottom layer is made of a softer material than the top." />
  <figcaption>Figure 7. A structure with the top face uniform and the bottom face thin and soft only where it folds. Thicknesses are example values given in the patent and are not to scale. <span class="src">US10897826B2 · drawing by this magazine</span></figcaption>
</figure>

The bottom layer was made of a material with a lower modulus than the top. BenQ also noted in its
patent background that methods varying the coating thickness between folding and non-folding parts had
been proposed. The idea is to resolve the trade-off only where it folds.

There is also the route of keeping the whole film soft. The Nature Communications coating in Figure 1
of the previous episode exceeded 7H pencil on CPI while its modulus measured by indentation was 2.25GPa,
tens of times lower than glass. A specimen made with 50 micrometers each of coating and CPI withstood
10,000 bends at a 1.5mm radius.

Cracks do not only form in the hard coat. Toyobo used a test that folds a film with a 10-micrometer hard
coat on 50-micrometer PET 200,000 times at a 3mm radius with the hard coat on the inside. The pencil
hardness pass line was 3H.

The defect Toyobo targeted in this filing is the easy-adhesion layer under the hard coat. Fine cracks
formed in this layer at the fold show up as rainbow-colored mura. So it varied the distribution of the
particles added to this layer by direction, and bounded the refractive index of the PET film within
ranges for each direction.

## Why did cover windows go to glass?

**The first foldable phones in 2019 used hard-coated colorless polyimide (CPI) film at the very top.**
The Galaxy Fold was reported to use Sumitomo Chemical's CPI, and earlier reports had Dongwoo Fine-Chem
handling that film's coating.

The Huawei Mate X used two layers of hard-coated CPI film. Kolon Industries was reported to have
supplied CPI for it along with the Motorola Razr.

A year later the tide turned. Samsung Display said in February 2020 that it had commercialized ultra-thin
glass (UTG) about 30 micrometers thick as the cover window for the Galaxy Z Flip. At the time, SCHOTT
supplied the UTG mother glass and Dowoo Insys processed it.

  <img src="/articles/2026-10-20-flexible-hard-coating/cover-window-timeline.svg" alt="A timeline of foldable cover windows. In 2019 the Galaxy Fold, Mate X and Razr used hard-coated CPI. In 2020 the Galaxy Z Flip first used UTG of about 30 micrometers. In 2021 Kolon CPI went into Xiaomi's first foldable and a Lenovo foldable PC. At the end of 2023 TrendForce counted UTG share in the latest foldable models at over 90 percent. In 2024 SK ie technology halted its flexible cover window business." />
  <figcaption>Figure 8. How cover windows moved from CPI to UTG. The 90% refers not to all foldables but to the latest models. <span class="src">Digital Daily · TheElec · iNews24 · KED Global · TrendForce · ZDNet Korea · drawing by this magazine</span></figcaption>
</figure>

Market research firm TrendForce counted UTG share in the latest foldable models at over 90% at the end
of 2023. SK ie technology said in February 2024 that it would wind down its flexible cover window
business, and in 2025 decided to sell that division to a Chinese company.

CPI did not disappear. Kolon CPI went into the cover window of Xiaomi's first foldable phone and
Lenovo's foldable PC in 2021. TrendForce also expected CPI to keep being used in mid-size foldables.

Reports in 2026 also said a structure laying a CPI protective film over UTG was under consideration. In
January that year, Kolon said nothing had been decided about supplying a specific customer's product.

**After the switch to glass, the place for the hard coat moved onto the glass.** Samsung Display and
Corning each filed structures with a hard coat on UTG. The product stack is confirmed by Samsung
Electronics' guidance.

The Galaxy Z screen is stacked, from the top, as a protective film, a protective layer, and the UTG.

  <figcaption>Figure 9. The body of the cover window became glass, with two layers of film on top of it. Undisclosed materials and thicknesses are not drawn. <span class="src">Samsung support page · Samsung Display · Korea IR Service · drawing by this magazine</span></figcaption>

The protective film is a consumable and is replaced at service centers. A company analysis report and
industry reports say that Sekyung Hi-Tech has supplied the protective layer directly on the UTG to
Samsung Display since 2019. Its material and thickness have not been disclosed.

Gathering the supply chain that can be confirmed from public sources, layer by layer, gives this.

| Layer | Supplier | Level of confirmation |
|---|---|---|
| CPI cover (2019) | Sumitomo Chemical (Galaxy Fold), Kolon Industries (Mate X · Razr) | Reports |
| CPI hard coat (2019) | Dongwoo Fine-Chem | Pre-launch reports |
| UTG mother glass | SCHOTT, with Corning as second supplier for the Galaxy Z Flip3 | Reports |
| UTG processing | Dowoo Insys | Reports |
| Protective layer on UTG | Sekyung Hi-Tech | Company analysis report · reports |
| Replaceable protective film | Not disclosed | |

**No layer has its material and thickness disclosed.** That is why Figure 9 gives only the names of
the layers and does not draw their thicknesses.

The job of the hard coat on UTG is the opposite of that on film. As we saw in the previous episode, a
layer softer than the glass takes impacts like a pen tip. So hardness has a ceiling.

Samsung Display set the indentation hardness of the hard coat on 30-70 micrometer UTG at 15-40HV. The
reason given is that **if hardness goes up, the window may break when folded**. The layer that lent
hardness on film has become, on glass, a layer that has to hold its hardness back.

In the same filing, Samsung Display put the hard coat thickness at 3-30 micrometers, preferably 6-14
micrometers. The hard coat does not take impacts alone. Two separate shock-absorbing layers of 10-60
micrometers and 5-50 micrometers are added.

That the top layer cannot be handled carelessly came to light with the first product. In April 2019
reports followed that Galaxy Fold screens failed within days, and Samsung warned that removing the
protective layer or attaching adhesives to the screen could cause damage.

## Closing Part 2: a single axis of surface roughness

Part 2, "Surface treatment," looked over seven episodes at the coatings placed on the outermost face of
the cover window: anti-fingerprint (AF), anti-glare (AG), anti-reflection and low reflection (AR·LR),
moth-eye, anti-static (AS), hard coat, and this episode's flexible hard coat.

Rewriting the seven episodes in one line each gives this.

- **AF** does not stop fingerprints from sticking but keeps them from spreading. Two products with nearly the same water contact angle split fourfold in abrasion resistance.
- **AG** does not reduce reflection but scatters it. When surface haze divided by internal haze is below 0.5, interference patterns appear; above 2, sparkle appears.
- **AR·LR** fill the gap of a refractive index of 1.23 with the pores of hollow silica or the interference of multiple layers.
- **Moth-eye** fills that gap with shape, but because the nano gaps trap fingerprint oil, it moved to places hands do not touch.
- **AS** sits not on the screen but on the protective film that gets peeled off.
- **HC** does a different job on each substrate under the same name, and **flexible HC** chooses its thickness between hardness and folding.

Stand these coatings on a single axis and it becomes surface roughness. **All of them deal with the
outermost face of the screen, and the job each takes on is decided by how smooth that face is kept.**

At the smoothest end is the transparent hard coat. One coating reported for flexible displays had an Ra
of 0.99 nanometers measured by atomic force microscopy. AR cancels reflection through the interference
of layers tens to a hundred nanometers thick, so the surface must be smooth, and AF sits on top as a
10-15 nanometer film.

In the few-nanometer range is the anti-Newton ring layer. Daicel claimed a film that blurs the
interference fringes between two films with Ra 3-15 nanometers while keeping clarity at 92% or more.
The worked examples were 6 nanometers and 9 nanometers.

In the tens to hundreds of nanometers range is AG. AG film with particles in the hard coat was Ra
50-150 nanometers, and acid-etched AG glass 80-580 nanometers. The rougher it is, the more the
reflected image blurs and the more the screen's clarity drops.

Moth-eye sits in the same hundreds of nanometers range but is different in character. It stands
protrusions about 300 nanometers high more densely than the wavelength of light, using the relief not
to scatter light but to change the refractive index gradually. But because those gaps trap oil, it
went to places hands do not touch rather than the outside of the screen people handle.

Only AS lies off this axis. That is because anti-static is not on the screen but on the protective film
peeled off during the process.

**The hard coat lies at the floor of this axis.** Kept smooth, it becomes the base for AR and AF; holding
particles, it becomes AG itself. On a foldable, as we saw in this episode, it becomes the layer that
chooses its thickness between hardness and folding.

Part 2 closes here. The next part goes down just beneath the surface, to the cover window.
