From October 26 to November 1, 2026, every newly published paper in 28 registered journals was collected. Of the 1,250 papers scanned, 109 leading to displays were selected, and 18 of those are listed below. Two in-depth articles chosen from the same window run separately.

What stood out this week was how color is made. When producing red, green and blue each at high efficiency stalls, research takes another route: a single white layer topped with color filters, a quantum dot conversion layer printed over a blue light source, or color shifted simply by changing the drive frequency. And electrochromics and structural color, never covered since our first issue, appeared three times in the same week.

## 1. A europium perovskite LED that emits white from a single material

Near-infrared, red and green perovskite LEDs have passed 30 percent external quantum efficiency, but blue has not kept pace. When efficiency differs by color, building a full-color active matrix is difficult and does not fit high-resolution patterning either. Jinghui Li and 14 others at the Wuhan National Laboratory for Optoelectronics, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, **went around the problem instead of tackling it head-on**: make a single white layer and top it with color filters.

The material is CsEuI₃ nanocrystals coated with mCP. The inorganic environment inside the crystal protects the blue emission of divalent europium, while the surface ligands alter the europium surroundings to add complementary emission at longer wavelengths. Two components from one material combine into white. With a color rendering index of 85.7 and color coordinates of (0.33, 0.34), it lands almost on the ideal white point.

The maximum external quantum efficiency is 8.3 percent and the maximum luminance 8,941 candelas per square meter. The device was integrated monolithically on a 256 by 256 active-matrix chip and went as far as displaying video and full-color images. The Hangzhou Institute for Advanced Study of the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, the Shanghai Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics and the University of Oxford, UK, also took part. (Nature Photonics)

## 2. A self-powered electrochromic device sharing a zinc mesh

Wearable displays face the constraint that power must be supplied from outside every time the screen is redrawn. Yaowu Li and 8 others at the University of Science and Technology of China and the Suzhou Institute of Nano-Tech and Nano-Bionics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, put the power source inside the device.

The structure is unusual. The upper layer is a display electrode made of tungsten and tungsten oxide, the lower layer a manganese dioxide energy-storage electrode, and a zinc mesh sandwiched between them links the two. Coloring is self-driven, and bleaching runs on electricity stored in the lower layer. It also produces several colors.

Electrochromics is a long-standing option for reflective displays, and a structure with a built-in power source overlaps with the position electronic paper is aiming for. (Nano Letters)

## 3. A viologen that draws three colors from one molecule

Viologens are molecules that change color under applied voltage. They are known to switch reversibly between two states at low voltage, but they usually stay at two colors. Lu Li and 2 others at Shaanxi Normal University went beyond that limit by inserting deprotonation as a gate.

Combining the voltage-driven electron transfer pathway with a proton-removal pathway increases the number of states from two to three or more. It produces multiple colors from a single molecule, and display devices, smart windows and adaptive mirrors are listed as applications. (Advanced Optical Materials)

## 4. A million-pixel structural color image cut into brass

Structural color is a possible replacement for dyes and pigments, but raising resolution and color gamut together is difficult. Changcheng Lin and 2 others at Harbin Institute of Technology, Shenzhen, proposed deterministically cutting blazed nanogratings into a brass surface.

The method cuts while dynamically varying a triangular profile, and each pixel measures 5 micrometers by 20 micrometers. It reproduced structural color images at the million-pixel scale. It is not a display screen, but on the side of making color from shape rather than material, it is a case that shows resolution and reproducibility together. (Laser & Photonics Reviews)

## 5. A quantum dot color conversion layer with printed microlenses

Quantum dot color conversion is a leading route to full color with micro-LEDs, but conversion layer efficiency is low. One cause is light trapped inside the layer by total internal reflection. **Sang Youn Lee and 5 others at the Korea Institute of Industrial Technology, Ansan**, inkjet-printed a microlens array of acrylic monomer on top of the conversion layer, improving angular light extraction. The BK21 FOUR ERICA-ACE Center at Hanyang University took part.

What stands out is that the entire process is done by inkjet. If the lenses are printed rather than shaped separately, they can be processed on the same line as the conversion layer. (Advanced Materials Technologies)

## 6. Red micro-LED wavelength held in place by an aluminum nitride interlayer

Producing red from InGaN requires a high indium content, which causes the emission wavelength to shift toward shorter wavelengths and the spectrum to broaden as current rises. In microdisplays this becomes a problem of color varying from pixel to pixel. Yang Su and 9 others suppressed that shift with an aluminum nitride interlayer and a low-temperature p-type gallium nitride design.

The device size is 5 micrometers by 5 micrometers. The structure was designed to preserve localized states and compared with a reference device. The first author's affiliation could not be confirmed in indexes or public bibliographic records, so it is not given. (Optics Express)

## 7. Deep-blue emitters approaching BT.2020 through a twisted structure

Yantao Deng and 4 others at Harbin Institute of Technology made two molecules with spiro-fluoreno-imidazole as the acceptor and spiro-acridine-fluorene as the donor. A para-phenylene bridge in the middle makes the molecule adopt a strongly twisted conformation.

Achieving efficiency and color purity together in deep blue is a long-standing OLED problem, and the BT.2020 standard narrows it further. This study aims at the same target as the violet-blue TADF article covered in depth in this issue, from a different direction. (ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces)

## 8. Bottlenecks of blue perovskite LEDs seen through diammonium structural modulation

This is a review by Shibing Zou and 6 others at Hubei Minzu University. It sorts the reasons blue lags behind near-infrared, red and green perovskite LEDs, which have reached efficiencies in the 30 percent range, into defect density, phase instability and behavior under electric field, and collects approaches that modulate structure with diammonium compounds.

It is a review rather than an original paper, but it shows in one place how far the bottlenecks of blue perovskites have been pinned down. (Laser & Photonics Reviews)

## 9. A tin perovskite LED built by suppressing self-doping

Tin perovskites are discussed as a lead-free emissive layer, but divalent tin oxidizes easily and crystallization is hard to control, leaving films rough and defect-rich. Chaohui Liang and 7 others at the Key Laboratory of Advanced Display and System Applications of the Ministry of Education, Shanghai University, suppressed self-doping with a multifunctional additive.

It is a material with a clear environmental rationale whose efficiency has lagged, and the approach handles oxidation and crystallization together with a single additive. (Applied Physics Letters)

## 10. Emissive nanocrystals patterned directly by lithography while stretchable

High-resolution stretchable displays need emitters that can be photopatterned and also stretch well. But patterning requires crosslinking the molecules into a network, and crosslinking reduces mobility so the film no longer stretches. **Sun Jae Park and 8 others at KAIST** resolved that trade-off with a benzophenone-based dual crosslinker. Seoul National University took part.

The point is that colloidal nanocrystal films were patterned by direct optical lithography while retaining intrinsic stretchability. It is worth watching on the process side of putting conversion or emissive layers on stretchable screens. (Nano Letters)

## 11. A silver nanowire electrode stretched with an inserted polyrotaxane

Silver nanowires are a leading material for stretchable transparent electrodes, but the modulus mismatch between rigid wires and soft polymer substrates limits flexibility. Xin Xu and 11 others at Tianjin University of Science and Technology inserted an acrylated polyrotaxane between the two.

A polyrotaxane is a molecule whose rings slide along an axle. The authors liken that sliding to a pulley and explain that it distributes stress. The work addresses the problem of the transparent electrode failing first in folding and stretching screens. (ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces)

## 12. Indium zinc oxide transparent electrodes compared across three solution methods

Danjela Kuscer and 3 others at the Jožef Stefan Institute, Slovenia, made In-Zn-O thin films by three methods, spin coating, inkjet printing and screen printing, and compared them. A separate solution was designed for each method.

Raising conductivity and transmittance together in transparent electrodes is familiar in vacuum processes but not easy in solution processes. Because it places three methods side by side with the same composition, the comparison is a reference for choosing a process. It looks at the same problem as the nanofiber electrode article covered in depth in issue 11, from the solution side. (Advanced Materials Technologies)

## 13. A polymer-dispersed liquid crystal smart window with voltage lowered by rubrene

Polymer-dispersed liquid crystals control scattering with voltage to make a window hazy or clear. However, the driving voltage is high and they do not block ultraviolet light. Rab Nawaz and 3 others at National Changhua University of Education, Taiwan, doped in rubrene to improve electro-optic performance and UV blocking together.

Smart windows are a different market from displays, but they share liquid crystal materials and cell processes. (Advanced Optical Materials)

## 14. A polarization-independent liquid crystal moiré metasurface

Electrically reconfigurable flat optics control wavefronts without mechanical motion. A moiré approach with liquid crystals can do this, but because liquid crystals are polarization-sensitive, only one linear polarization component could be handled. Yongxiang Qu and 6 others at Zhejiang University proposed a polarization-independent all-solid-state structure.

**This paper was in accepted status among last issue's candidates.** It was formally published on October 30 and entered this window. However, it is a study shown by numerical analysis, not by device fabrication. (Optics Express)

## 15. Electrically matched silicon nanowire and IGZO complementary transistors

Monolithic 3D complementary transistors stack devices vertically in the same area to raise logic density. That requires the p-channel and n-channel to each perform well and to match each other electrically. Le Weng and 5 others at Nanjing University paired p-type silicon nanowires with n-type IGZO to meet that condition.

It is a picture of a display backplane material stacked together with silicon. Together with the second in-depth article in this issue, which used IGZO as a photodetector interlayer, two cases of the same material used outside displays appeared in one week. (ACS Nano)

## 16. A quantum dot device that shifts color by drive frequency alone

The device made by Shaohong Wu and 6 others at Fuzhou University has an extremely simple structure: indium tin oxide, quantum dots and an insulating layer stacked together. Changing the frequency of the voltage changes the emission color.

What stands out is that it changes color by touching only the drive conditions, without adding layers or splitting pixels. The authors list uses in lighting and optical communication as well as display. (Applied Physics Letters)

## 17. Thin films for flexible electronics printed on a liquid surface

Soft thin films, the foundation of flexible electronics, must have uniform thickness and no residual stress, and it is hard to maintain yield through transferring them without defects after fabrication. Mengtian Yin and 5 others at the University of Virginia, USA, presented a method of printing thin films on a liquid surface.

A 3D-printed structure is spread in a controlled way on the liquid to obtain a uniform film. It is a process paper, worth a look wherever transfer yield is at stake. (Science Advances)

## 18. A quantum dot optical synapse transistor on silicon

Zhongyu Liu and 6 others at the Chinese Academy of Sciences built a dual-gate synaptic transistor combining colloidal quantum dots with a silicon-on-insulator channel. They emphasize compatibility with complementary metal-oxide semiconductor processes.

It is part of the push to do photodetection and neuromorphic computing in a single device, where existing optical synapse devices covered a narrow wavelength range or were hard to integrate on silicon. It connects with the direction of placing sensors inside the screen and processing signals right there. (Nano Letters)

## 19. What the collection process caught

Of the 109 candidates in this window, 9 had empty abstracts. They split along the same lines as last issue.

Four Optics Express papers have pages, volume and issue all blank. This is not a matter of access rights; they are not yet published. Just as item 14 above was in that state last issue and was formally published this time, they will be reviewed in a later issue once published.

The Nature Photonics paper in item 1 above was a different case. **The index had no abstract, but the paper page did.** An automated request returned 303 and the content could not be seen; only by opening it directly in a browser did the abstract and affiliations appear. Had we dropped it based on the index alone, we would have missed this week's top-tier paper.

The remaining four, a biology paper, a magnetic materials study, a molecular spectroscopy paper and a review on biomimetics, are far from displays and were not checked separately.
