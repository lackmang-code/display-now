Hold a phone at an angle without turning it on and the fingerprints show. Rub it on a shirt a
few times and it mostly comes clean. Set against the grease on a drinking glass, which a dry
cloth barely lifts, that is an odd result. The same oil from the same finger, and a different
outcome.

What makes the difference is a layer of about 10 nanometers sitting on top of the cover window.
The industry calls it AF, anti-fingerprint coating. The name reads as though it keeps
fingerprints off, but that is not what the layer actually does.

This episode walks one layer from material to specification. What it is made of, how it is laid
down, what it is measured with, and why it wears out. Along the way one conclusion lands
straight in practice. **An AF coating cannot be chosen by contact angle.** Why that is so is the
spine of this piece.

## 1. What comes off a fingertip

Two things transfer when a finger touches glass. One is sweat from the eccrine glands, the
other oil from the sebaceous glands.

Eccrine secretion is mostly water. The composition given in the literature is **98.5 to 99.5
percent water and 0.5 to 1.5 percent solids**, with salts, amino acids and urea among the
solids. Water evaporates and leaves a thin film of salt. So it is not the water side that
decides how a fingerprint looks.

What stays is the oil. Sebum is made of fatty acids, wax esters and squalene, and its surface
tension is low. Hydrocarbon oils generally fall in the range of **20 to 31 millinewtons per
meter**. For comparison, water is 72.8 under the same conditions. n-Hexadecane, often used in
testing, sits at 27.5, which places it well for standing in for sebum.

The glass on the other side has high surface energy. Cleanly washed glass wets so well that
even water lies almost flat on it. When a low surface tension liquid meets a high surface energy
solid the outcome is settled. The liquid spreads. Rather than staying a droplet where the finger
landed, it becomes a thin film and creeps sideways.

That raises a second question. Why is a spread oil film visible at all? The oil itself is
colorless and transparent.

It is visible because the film has a refractive index different from the glass and its thickness
is uneven. Light crossing two interfaces, air to film and film to glass, produces reflections,
and because film thickness varies from place to place so does the amount of reflection. Uneven
gloss is the result. That is why literature on fingerprint visibility also lists reducing the
**difference in gloss and refractive index between residue and surface** as a countermeasure.

Put together, the fingerprint problem is not a problem of how much lands but a problem of **how
far it spreads**. The same amount beaded up is a few dots. Spread into a film, it hazes one whole
side of the screen. And that distinction explains exactly what an AF coating is.

## 2. What an AF coating actually does

This layer cannot stop oil from landing. When a finger touches, the oil transfers all the same.
**What changes is the shape the transferred oil takes.**

The method is to lower the surface energy on the solid side. Once it is low, spreading buys the
liquid nothing, so the liquid settles the other way, shrinking its contact area and beading up.

There is a reference line for how far it has to be lowered. The **critical surface tension** set
out by Zisman is the boundary below which a liquid completely wets a solid. The values by exposed
functional group are well known.

| Group exposed at the surface | Critical surface tension (mN/m) |
|---|---|
| CF3 | 6 |
| CF2 | 17 |
| CH3 | 22 to 24 |
| CH2 | 31 |

These values are the lower half of Figure 1. Set next to the oils above, why it has to be
fluorine is clear at a glance. The oil to be dealt with is 20 to 31. A surface covered in
hydrocarbon chains lands at 22 to 24 even as CH3, overlapping the surface tension range of oil.
Depending on the oil, it simply wets. **CF3 at the end of a fluorinated chain, by contrast, is 6.**
The gap against the opponent opens wide.

That is also where the difference in difficulty between repelling water and repelling oil comes
from. Water is 72.8, so almost any low energy surface beads it. There are several routes to
hydrophobicity. Oil, though, has less than half the surface tension of water, so reaching
oleophobicity requires **a surface densely covered in CF3**. Surfaces that repel water but not oil
are common; the reverse is rare.

Manufacturer technical data shows the result in numbers. Daikin's AF material Optool on
chemically strengthened glass, compared with bare glass, looks like this.

| Item | Bare glass | Optool treated |
|---|---|---|
| Water contact angle | under 10 degrees | 109 degrees |
| n-Hexadecane contact angle | under 10 degrees | 65 degrees |
| Kinetic friction coefficient | 0.15 | 0.08 |

Under 10 degrees for both water and oil on bare glass means they effectively lie flat. After
treatment water stands at 109 degrees as a bead and hexadecane reaches 65. The value practice
looks at first is not water but **the hexadecane side**. The fingerprint problem is oil, not water.

And contact angle alone is half the story. A droplet standing and a droplet rolling are different
questions. The value published alongside is the **sliding angle**, which for UD509 is 6 degrees for
both water and n-hexadecane. Tilt the plate by 6 degrees and the droplet runs off.

That is what the sense of "it wipes off" really is. Oil bonded broadly across a face has to be
physically pushed away, but oil beaded up and ready to roll is swept off with one pass of a shirt
cuff. **What an AF coating sells is not resistance to landing but ease of removal.**

## 3. The material: a molecule of head and tail

The material behind this behavior is mostly the **PFPE silane** family. PFPE stands for
perfluoropolyether, a polymer in which every hydrogen on the carbon chain has been replaced by
fluorine with oxygen inserted at regular intervals. Daikin labels its own product as modified
PFPE dissolved in a fluorinated solvent.

Divide the molecule by function and there are two parts.

**The tail is the fluorinated chain.** Fluorine has high electronegativity and low polarizability,
so the dispersion forces pulling on neighboring molecules are weak. When these chains line up
facing outward they make the CF3 surface from the previous section, a face in the 6 mN/m range of
critical surface tension. The oxygen inserted along the chain is there to give flexibility; a
chain that bends well lies and stands evenly on the surface and builds a layer without gaps.

What matters here is not only that the chain exists but its **orientation**. If the fluorinated
chain lies flat on the surface, what is exposed outward becomes the CF2 side and critical surface
tension rises to 17. The terminal CF3 has to stand densely facing outward for the face to
approach 6. That is why the same material can perform differently when deposition and curing go
wrong.

**The head is the silane group.** This side bonds to the glass surface. Glass surfaces carry
silanol groups, and once the silane group has hydrolyzed it condenses with them, producing an
oxygen-mediated bond. With only a tail the molecule sits on the surface until it is wiped away;
the head is what keeps it there. Humidity appears among the curing conditions in Section 4 because
of this reaction.

The layer is thin. Manufacturer data puts it at the level of **10 to 15 nanometers** and states
that at that thickness it does not distort the optical properties of the glass. In practice the
visible transmission spectrum nearly overlaps bare glass with little ripple across wavelength. It
is not a layer that designs reflection the way an AR coating does; optically it is treated as
absent. That contrasts with AG and AR, layers whose purpose is optical, which later episodes will
take up.

The supply form is in the data as well. It is a liquid with 20 percent polymer dissolved in
solvent, density 1.43 grams per cubic centimeter, appearance a pale yellow or clear liquid.
Unopened shelf life is 1 year, storage between 5 and 25 degrees, away from direct sunlight.

Routes that avoid fluorine have been studied as well. Organosiloxane self-assembled monolayers
aiming at AF behavior are the representative attempt. As the critical surface tension table shows,
however, a hydrocarbon surface is 22 to 24 even as CH3 and overlaps the range of oil. The
structural reason it is hard to place such surfaces **alongside fluorinated materials on
oleophobicity** is that, even where hydrophobicity is reached. This is taken up again in Section 8.

## 4. How it is laid down

The same material can be laid down two ways. Manufacturer data states that both are supported.

### Wet

Make a solution and apply it. Spray, dip, spin and flow coating are all used. The procedure the
data sets out runs as follows.

Clean the substrate first. It specifies that **plasma, UV and ozone, and chemical cleaning** are
generally effective. This step is not a formality, and the reason is the bonding structure from
Section 3. If the silanol groups the head would bond to are covered in organics, no bond forms,
and a molecule that failed to bond may contribute to the initial contact angle but disappears at
the first rub.

Next is dilution. It is thinned with a fluorinated solvent such as a hydrofluoroether or
perfluorohexane, and the starting point the data offers is **0.1 percent by weight of polymer,
which is a 200-fold dilution**. That means taking the 20 percent stock and thinning it 200 times.
Spray quantity is given with a typical value of **30 to 60 grams per square meter**.

The concentration goes this low because the target is close to a monolayer. Laying it on thicker
does not improve performance. The number of sites available for bonding on the surface is fixed,
and what piles up above them becomes a layer resting without bonds, which instead produces early
wear.

### Dry (PVD)

Vacuum deposition. The material is made into pellets and heated to vaporize inside PVD equipment.
Here the data adds one condition, and this sentence connects straight to Section 7.

> For best results, an **SiO2 adhesion layer should be deposited on the substrate first**, before
> Optool is applied.

The AF material bonds with silanol groups on a glass surface, so if the substrate is not glass or
its surface condition is uneven, the sites to bond to are simply lacking. Laying an SiO2 layer
first creates uniform bonding sites above it. In real cover windows AF often goes not on bare
glass but on top of an optical multilayer such as AR, and in such stacks what the top layer is
finished with governs the life of the AF. A separate patent covering exactly the configuration of
AF over optical and scratch-resistant films exists for the same reason.

### Curing

Both routes go through curing. The data offers two conditions.

| Condition | Temperature | Humidity | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low temperature, high humidity | 60 degrees | 90 percent relative humidity | 1 hour |
| High temperature, ambient humidity | 150 degrees | ambient | 30 minutes |

And it states flatly that **curing is required to form a chemical bond with the substrate**. That
humidity appears among the conditions stands out. For the silane to bond with the surface,
hydrolysis has to happen first, and that reaction needs water. It reads as though a low
temperature is compensated with humidity while raising the temperature lets ambient humidity carry
it. It also says that managing curing as a matter of temperature alone can let results drift with
the season.

After curing, unbonded residue is wiped or washed off with a fluorinated solvent. Molecules
resting on the surface without bonds come off first, and initial performance turns erratic.

A process constraint is listed as well. This material **decomposes and becomes hazardous when
heated continuously above 200 degrees**. That sets the temperature ceiling for whatever process
follows the AF step. Cross that line while designing a thermal or bonding process downstream and
what is lost is not only performance but safety.

### Which route to take

The two are separated not by performance but by **production conditions**.

Wet, and spray in particular, is favorable on cost. The material needed to form a monolayer is
small to begin with, and because the solution is sprayed from a sealed vessel, evaporative loss is
also small. Against dip coating, the volume of solution required is smaller and the energy to
drive off solvent is lower. It is also cited as able to lay a relatively uniform film on uneven or
rough substrates.

Vacuum deposition is strong elsewhere. It has long been used where precise thickness control is
required, as with optical components, and as noted it can lay the SiO2 adhesion layer first in the
same flow. If AR multilayers are already being stacked in vacuum, carrying on to AF is natural.

Two burdens come with it. One is **curvature**. If the substrate is bent, deposition rate varies by
location, so **masking** is needed to shield the fast regions and match the slow ones. Masking
complicates chamber tooling and lowers the overall deposition rate. This burden grew as cover
windows moved from flat to 2.5D and 3D curves.

The other is **large area**. As area grows, the required vacuum level and pump-down time grow with
it. Beyond phone cover glass, at automotive covers or notebook panels, this constraint binds first.

### Handling and storage

The data also shows the material to be moisture sensitive. Storage is specified between 5 and 25
degrees away from direct sunlight, and after use the instruction is to **close the cap to prevent
evaporation and moisture exposure, and to minimize the air in the bottle before resealing or
replace it with a dry inert gas such as nitrogen or argon**.

This instruction follows from the bonding chemistry of Section 3. Silane groups hydrolyze on
contact with water. That reaction is only useful when it happens at the surface; if it happens
inside the bottle first, the molecules condense with each other. The unopened shelf life of 1 year
comes from the same place. **Adding humidity in the curing condition and removing it in the storage
condition** is the basis of handling this material.

## 5. What it is measured with

The specification of this layer is not optical. It is made of surface properties and touch.

### Contact angle and hysteresis

**Contact angle** is measured with two liquids, water and n-hexadecane. In practice, though,
contact angle is not a single value. The **advancing angle** measured while growing the droplet
differs from the **receding angle** measured while shrinking it, and the gap is called **contact
angle hysteresis**.

Instrument makers' literature gives two reasons this matters. First, hysteresis on the order of
tens of degrees signals **chemical non-uniformity or roughness-related non-uniformity** in the
coating. Second, **wiping performance correlates with hysteresis**, and the smaller the hysteresis
the better it wipes.

And there is one more point on this layer, the most practical of all. As an AF coating degrades it
is reported that **the static contact angle holds high while hysteresis rises first**. Watch only
the static contact angle and the number looks fine while the coating is already going bad. The
problems of Sections 6 and 7 announce themselves here.

### Sliding angle and friction coefficient

**Sliding angle** is the tilt at which a droplet begins to roll. If contact angle is a static
value, this one is dynamic, and it maps directly onto wiping in real use.

**Kinetic friction coefficient** corresponds to the feel of a finger sliding across. The
measurement conditions the manufacturer discloses are specific. Labthink FPT-1 equipment, 200 gram
load, 200 millimeters per minute, paper friction pad. Setting friction curves side by side, what
the same data points at is not only magnitude. On bare glass the curve chatters like saw teeth; on
the coated face it is smooth. **Stick-slip**, sliding and catching in turn, is reduced, and that is
the smoothness a finger feels crossing the screen.

Worth marking here. **Finger feel is managed as a specification item.** A domain easily waved away
as perceived quality has come down, on this layer, to a number called friction coefficient. As will
be seen later, this value splits by more than two times even between materials in the same family.

### How the fingerprint itself is reproduced

Contact angle and friction coefficient are measured with surrogate liquids. The final performance,
whether a fingerprint wipes off, has to be measured with a fingerprint, and human fingers are not
reproducible. So artificial fingerprints are made.

The method set out in patents and papers runs like this. An artificial secretion of defined
composition is prepared and transferred to the surface **by stamping with a silicone stamp at a
defined force**. A heated solution blending glyceryl trioleate with artificial sebum is described.
Removability is evaluated **by wiping with a cloth of defined mass at defined force and speed**.
Where quantification is needed, a known amount of sebum is deposited with a spraying device and the
actual deposited mass is verified with UV-visible spectroscopy and microgravimetry.

That is the measurement system of this layer. Three tiers, put simply. Contact angle and hysteresis
measure **the property of the material**; sliding angle and friction coefficient measure **the feel
in use**; and the artificial fingerprint test measures **the final performance**. That the three do
not always point the same way is the story of the next section.

## 6. How long it lasts, and how to read that number

The life of an AF layer is measured by abrasion testing. The widely used method rubs the surface
with steel wool and counts the reciprocations until contact angle falls below a set level.

The conditions Daikin's European technical data gives are these.

> Maximum number of reciprocations while the water contact angle stays at or above 100 degrees.
> Steel wool #0000, load 1 kilogram per square centimeter, stroke 6 centimeters, speed 60 per minute.

The same data places two of its own products side by side, and that table is the core of this
episode.

| Item | Optool UD509 | Optool DSX |
|---|---|---|
| Water contact angle | 116 degrees | (not listed in the table) |
| Water sliding angle | 6 degrees | 8 degrees |
| Steel wool abrasion | **24,000 cycles** | **6,000 cycles** |
| Kinetic friction coefficient | 0.03 | 0.07 |

The water contact angle in the DSX data issued by the US affiliate was 109 degrees. UD509 in the
European data is 116 degrees. **A difference of 7 degrees.** Yet in the same table the abrasion
counts open up **four times**, at 24,000 and 6,000. The friction coefficient differs by more than
two times as well, at 0.03 and 0.07.

Here is the conclusion running through this episode. **An AF coating cannot be chosen by contact
angle.** The initial contact angle says how well this layer works at the start and says almost
nothing about how long it goes on working. In real use it is the latter that matters. Nobody
complains that fingerprints will not wipe off a new phone.

The hysteresis from Section 5 layers onto this. The static contact angle responds late as
degradation proceeds, and hysteresis rises first. Manage the state of this layer by static contact
angle alone and **you are wrong when choosing a product and late when tracking degradation**.

The same data shows the trap in handling numbers. The US affiliate's DSX sheet lists steel wool
abrasion at **4,000 cycles** with no test conditions alongside. In the European comparison table
the same product is 6,000. Rather than one being wrong, they are better read as **values from tests
under different conditions**. Change the load, stroke, speed and above all the contact angle at
which the test is called finished, and the count moves however you like. Drop the criterion from
100 degrees to 90 and the count for the same sample rises a great deal.

So the rule for reading data on this layer is one line. **An abrasion count with no conditions
attached cannot be used for comparison.** If you are handed a sheet setting two companies' products
side by side on counts alone, that table is not evidence but marketing. Check first whether the
conditions on both sides are the same.

The caveat the manufacturer attaches itself is carried over here as well. The property values above
are representative, not guaranteed, and are stated as unsuitable for use as specifications.

## 7. Why it wears out

To see why lifetimes split like this, look at where the wear actually happens.

The intuitive picture is that the coating rubs off. A thin film is worn away bit by bit and where
it is gone, fingerprints start to stay. But this layer is about 10 nanometers thick and is closer
to a structure of single molecules bonded standing on glass. "Rubbing off" does not fit cleanly.

Studies of degradation in this family of coatings point somewhere else. Rather than the silane head
coming away from the glass, **the fluorinated chain side breaks**. In particular the **Si-C bond**
linking the PFPE chain to the silane group is identified as the weakest link in the chain.
Analyzing surfaces before and after abrasion with XPS to track silicon and fluorine content is the
method used in such work.

If that account holds, the picture inverts. An abraded surface is left **with the head still there
and only the tail cut away**. The number of molecules attached to the surface has not dropped much,
but the fluorinated chains facing outward have shortened or gone, so only the function of lowering
surface energy is lost.

Hold the critical surface tension table from Section 3 against this and the path of degradation
appears. An intact layer has CF3 facing outward, making a face in the 6 mN/m range. When chains
break, CF2 is exposed in their place or the silane-side structure is revealed, and the effective
surface energy rises. A partially broken surface becomes **an uneven face mixing CF3 regions with
regions that are not**, and as Section 5 showed, the signature of such a face is exactly rising
hysteresis. The static contact angle still reads high thanks to the CF3 regions that remain, but the
droplet catches when it moves.

The numbers of the previous section are explained here. The initial contact angle measures how well
the tails are lined up; the abrasion count measures how long those tails stay attached. **They
measure different things**, so it is only natural that they do not move together. The gap between
116 and 109 degrees is a difference in tail arrangement, but the gap between 24,000 and 6,000 comes
from chain structure, degree of crosslinking, and the density of the sites holding on.

The SiO2 adhesion layer recommendation from Section 4 runs along the same line. Make bonding sites
dense and uniform and the same material lasts longer. Reports that samples with an alumina or
silica adhesion layer held their contact angle longer in abrasion testing do not run against this
direction. And it explains why the cleaning conditions of Section 3 and the curing conditions of
Section 4 are written out so specifically. **The life of this layer is largely decided not at the
moment the material is chosen but in the process that attaches it.**

That said, the mechanism described in this section rests on academic literature and has not been
confirmed against primary data with conditions and numbers stated, the way a manufacturer technical
data sheet does. The account that the Si-C bond is the weakest link, and that chain scission is the
main path, should be read as **the direction studies point to** and is hard to use as grounds for
asserting the actual degradation path of a specific product.

### Rubbing is not the only path to wear

The steel wool test looks only at mechanical abrasion. In real use the paths that break this layer
down are several, and the literature names at least four together.

**First, mechanical abrasion.** The chain scission path seen so far. Fingers, clothing and grit in a
pocket belong here.

**Second, hydrolysis.** Hydrophobic coatings are noted as tending to fail by hydrolysis after
exposure to harsh solvent conditions. The bond linking surface and coating is an oxygen-mediated
structure, so that bond itself can react with water and run backward. The storage instructions of
Section 4 are the other face of this property.

**Third, ultraviolet light.** Long exposure to sunlight can bring yellowing or degradation, so UV
stability is separately required for outdoor use. It is an item that **binds in automotive displays
before phones**. A display above a dashboard takes high temperature and strong ultraviolet at the
same time in summer.

**Fourth, adhesion failure.** Fluorinated compounds are noted as adhering poorly to glass
substrates, which can lead to delamination under mechanical stress or thermal cycling. This is a
different failure from chain scission. It is closer to the whole layer coming away, which is why the
SiO2 adhesion layer and the cleaning conditions of Section 4 matter.

Resistance to cleaning agents reads differently across sources. On one side there is the general
statement that environments exposed to detergents, solvents and industrial atmospheres require a
chemically inert coating; on the other there is a report that **rubbing with isopropyl alcohol left
the water and methylene iodide contact angles unaffected**. It is best read as varying with
composition, and in fact a patent states that **including a compound with a silazane group improves
alcohol resistance**.

Put the four together and the conclusion of Section 6 is confirmed once more. A steel wool count
measures **one of the four paths**. A high number is no guarantee of surviving 3 years in an
automotive environment. Which is why the literature on the durability of this layer converges on one
diagnosis. **Most AF coatings have excellent initial performance and degrade quickly under real use
conditions. Durability is the bottleneck to commercialization.**

## 8. What is riding on this layer now

### Supply structure

A small number of chemical companies make this material. Names cited together as suppliers of
commercial display AF coatings include Daikin, Shin-Etsu Chemical, AGC Chemicals, 3M, Solvay, PPG,
Schott, Dow and SDC Technologies. Among them Daikin's Optool family has published technical data,
which is why the numbers in this episode could be drawn from it. Values from the other companies are
not published in a form comparable under the same conditions. That is where the rule of Section 6
binds directly in procurement practice.

In Korea, cover glass maker JNTC announced in 2024-02 that it had independently developed
prior technology for AF coating. The announcement states that abrasion resistance improved more than
20 times against a global customer's criterion, and that where the AF effect of existing products
lasts several months, theirs holds 5 to 6 years or more. This is a company announcement with no
disclosed comparison target or test conditions, so by the principle set out above it should be
handled as a number with no conditions attached.

### Regulation

What is riding most heavily on this layer right now is not performance but regulation.

The universal PFAS restriction under discussion in the European REACH framework centers on
prohibiting non-essential PFAS in articles above 0.1 percent by weight, with the scope of covered
substances cited at over 10,000. The European Chemicals Agency began final consultation on the
proposed restriction on 2026-03-26, and the final opinion of the Committee for Socio-Economic
Analysis is expected at the end of 2026.

The common rebuttal is that polymers are exempt. What analyses of this discussion point out in
common, though, is that **it is not enough for a supplier sheet to say polymer exemption**. Low
molecular weight fractions, or fragments that can come away during use, may fall within scope.

Lay the mechanism of Section 7 over this and the problem becomes clear. If the degradation of this
layer proceeds by chains breaking, then where the broken fragments go becomes a regulatory question.
What makes the performance is the fluorinated chain, and what comes away as it wears is also the
fluorinated chain.

This is where the non-fluorinated approach mentioned in Section 3 returns. Research aims at low
surface energy without fluorine, as with organosiloxane self-assembled monolayers, but as the
critical surface tension table shows, a hydrocarbon surface is 22 to 24 even as CH3 and overlaps the
range of oil. There are several routes to repelling water, but **fluorine was overwhelming at
repelling oil**, which is the fact seen in Section 2, and it is what makes the regulatory response
hard.

### What remains

This layer is 10 nanometers thick and, counted as cost, is not a large share of the cover window
process. Yet it is the only layer a user touches every time they touch the screen, and when it wears
the user knows at once. And the foundation of its material now sits in the middle of a regulatory
debate.

Reduced to three lines, what this episode marked out is this. AF does not stop fingerprints, it
stops them spreading. What does that work is CF3 standing outward, and wear begins where those
chains break. And so **the initial contact angle does not represent the performance of this layer.**

The next episode takes up a different layer on the same face. A layer where the problem is not
fingerprints but light, AG. It works by roughening the surface on purpose to scatter reflection, and
it runs head-on into the demand seen in this episode that the surface be smooth and uniform. That is
where the demands on one face begin to collide.
