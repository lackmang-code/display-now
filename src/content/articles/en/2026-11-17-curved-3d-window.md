## Four Manufacturing Routes for Curved Covers

There are four ways to make a curved cover. 2.5D grinds the edges of a flat sheet round. 3D thermoforming heats the glass and presses it into a mold. Cold forming bends a flat sheet that has already been strengthened at room temperature and holds it in a frame. Plastic is either heated and pressed or injected as resin into a mold.

Curvature is not the only thing that separates the four routes. What each leaves in the part is different. 2.5D leaves no bending stress. Thermoforming leaves no stress but leaves warp after strengthening. Cold forming carries bending stress for the entire life of the part. With plastic, the hard surface layer has to follow the deformation of the bulk.

## Grinding and Polishing 2.5D Edges

2.5D has no standard definition. Corning defined 2.5D in its own patent as cover glass whose front edge is curved, noting that it can be made by mechanical polishing and feels smoother to the touch than 2D. 3D is glass whose edges bend so that the sheet leaves the plane. Lens Technology wrote in a 2017 filing that glass with a flat bottom and only an arched top surface is 2.5D, and that 3D can be regarded as 2.5D with its bottom hollowed out concave. This investigation found no public figures for the arc height or radius.

The process order follows that of cover glass in general. A 2017 review in the American Ceramic Society Bulletin summarized it as cutting the sheet, beveling by CNC, drilling holes, polishing the edges and then chemically strengthening. The Lens Technology patent stacks 2.5D glass and polishes the sides with a brush wheel, inserting 0.4 to 1.5 mm polishing leather between the convex faces that touch.

There is a reason strengthening comes after machining. A Hoya patent states that it is preferable to strengthen after machining the recess, explaining that strengthened glass contains a tensile layer that balances the surface compression. If strengthening comes first, machining forces disturb that tensile layer. The use of 2.5D is tied to the smartphone body. When Apple announced the iPhone 6 in 2014, it described the anodized aluminum body as meeting the formed display glass to create a seamless surface. Apple did not, however, use the term 2.5D and did not disclose the machining method.

Rounding the edges has one more reason besides feel: compression is lost at the edge during strengthening. Apple's 2009 filing gave the reduction in surface compression at the edge as about 22% for a square edge, about 16% for a 0.15 mm chamfer, and about 4% for a rounded edge with a radius of about 25% of the thickness. Corning claimed a sheet whose surface compression at a rounded edge is at least 78% of that at the center of the face. Rounding carries the compression through to the edge.

2.5D also has the drawback of a thinner edge. Corning's strengthening patent calls glass whose thickness decreases toward the edge 2.5D and names it as a case where strengthening of the periphery is especially important. This is where curved surfaces stop being a machining problem of flat sheets. Bending further cannot be done by grinding. Corning wrote that making 3D by grinding is limited to shapes with little stock removal and to simple cylindrical or spherical convex surfaces.

## Viscosity and Temperature in 3D Thermoforming

The criterion for thermoforming is not a single temperature but the viscosity of the glass. Corning's 2011 filing claimed heating the sheet to a temperature corresponding to a viscosity of 10⁷ to 10¹¹ poise and conforming it to a mold, with the mold held 100 to 200 °C below the glass. A 2014 filing divided the range: shallow curves at 10⁹ to 10¹¹ poise, and tight bends and sharp corners at 10⁷ to 10⁸ poise.

Converted to temperature, the values vary by composition. In Corning's property sheet, Gorilla Glass 2 has a softening point of 895 °C and an annealing point of 653 °C. That is why the patents state viscosity rather than temperature. A Corning mold patent states that forming above about 600 °C creates surface defects.

In smartphones, Samsung Electronics led this route. Samsung said in 2015 that both curved sides of the Galaxy S6 edge were made by 3D thermoforming. Published research also shows the width of the temperature window. A 2024 paper on 0.3 mm glass for smartwatches set 615 to 625 °C as the process window in a graphite mold, and a 2025 paper calculated maximum simulated residual stress of 26.30 MPa at 610 °C, 17.95 MPa at 630 °C, and 17.60 MPa at 640 °C. Raising the temperature by a few tens of degrees lowers residual stress but moves closer to the surface-defect region by the same amount.

Lowering the viscosity makes complex shapes easier but damages the surface. Corning wrote that at low viscosity the glass reboils and develops orange peel, and that marks from the vacuum holes in the mold are imprinted on the glass. The countermeasure is to finish the mold surface to below Ra 10 nm and coat it with glassy carbon. Forming pressure also has upper and lower limits. Pressing too hard thins the glass and causes optical distortion; too little pressure leaves wrinkles in complex areas.

AGC's 2017 filing proposed varying the mold gap by location. The gap at the flat portion is matched to within 0.02 mm of the glass thickness, and the gap at the curved portion is widened by up to 0.10 to 0.50 mm. In the examples, 0.5 mm glass was heated to a viscosity of 10⁹·⁵ Pa·s and pressed at up to 0.55 MPa. Comparative Example 1, with little gap variation, had a retardation of 18.14 nm per mm, while Examples 1 to 3 had 5.36 to 6.66 nm. The filing considered 16 nm/mm or less to give no visible wrinkles at the four corners.

The mold material must match the glass in thermal expansion. AGC pointed out that a carbon mold has a lower coefficient of thermal expansion than glass, so the mold and glass change dimensions differently during pressing. It gave 50×10⁻⁷/K or more for the convex mold, with a difference from the glass of 20×10⁻⁷/K or less, as the preferred range; Example 4 used glass at 72×10⁻⁷ and a convex mold at 70×10⁻⁷.

Mass production is a line of hot stations. A patent from a Shenzhen equipment maker described a conventional 13-station hot-bending machine as 4 heating, 3 forming, 2 annealing and 4 cooling stations. At 80 seconds per station, forming takes 240 seconds or more and heating 320 seconds. The filing shortened the forming stations to 50 seconds, completing forming in 150 seconds, and added heating stations.

Molds are consumables. In Corning's 2013 filing, coated Inconel 718 molds had an average life of 100 to 150 cycles, while uncoated Ni201 molds exceeded 1,000 cycles. Graphite oxidizes at glass-forming temperatures in the 400 to 900 °C range, pitting its surface, and the marks transfer to the glass. Soft grades are easy to machine but wear quickly; hard grades are the opposite. No published value was found for the cycle life of graphite molds.

The cost was high. Lens Technology told investors in 2017 that 3D curved glass was priced at 3 to 5 times 2.5D and that its yield fell short of 2D and 2.5D levels. At the time, it owned more than 400 hot-bending machines.

## Warp of 3D Glass from Ion-Exchange Strengthening

3D glass is formed, edge-finished and then ion-exchanged. This order is what causes warp. Ion exchange pushes large potassium ions into small sodium sites, so the surface layer expands. A flat sheet only grows about 0.04% in dimension, but 3D glass warps 10 to 150 μm depending on shape. Corning wrote that customer tolerances are typically ±100 μm. Warp alone can consume the entire tolerance.

Corning's answer is to cut the mold deliberately wrong. A 2013 filing predicts the deformation that strengthening will cause with a computer model, distorts the mold in advance by that amount, and states that one compensation reduces deviation from CAD to about ±10 μm. Claim 1 is the act of making a mold based on a model that includes ion exchange through the edges, because the edge effect is often larger than all the rest combined.

Coatings enter the same calculation. Another Corning filing states that residual stress in functional coatings warps parts out of dimensional tolerance, and more so the thinner the glass. So the warp from strengthening and the warp from coating are built into the mold together. In the dependent claims, coating compressive stress is 100 to 950 MPa and thickness is 5 nm to 5 μm.

Strength data for curved surfaces are scarce. In an example in a Corning patent, 3D glass 0.8 mm thick with a 3 mm bend radius was single ion-exchanged (surface compression CS 787 MPa, depth of layer DOL 95 μm) and dropped flat; it survived four drops from 200 cm. The curved portion was not measured separately, however, and there was no corner drop. A 2016 finite element analysis by Corning researchers put the bend angle favorable for drops at 0 to 45° and the radius at 3.8 mm or more. No published measured distribution of CS and DOL in the curved portion was found.

The thickness at the bend affects both strength and optics. Corning wrote that variation in wall thickness at the bend leads to optical distortion and loss of strength, and addressed covers that keep thickness variation below ±100 μm. This means that the thinning from excessive forming pressure noted earlier also leads to loss of strength.

## Four-Corner Double Curvature and Panel Lamination

Even with the glass finished, the panel still has to be laminated. The long and short sides bend in one direction only, so a flat panel can follow them. The corners where two sides meet bend in two directions at once, leaving excess area, and wrinkles form there. Visionox researchers described these locations at SID 2025 as surfaces with nonzero Gaussian curvature.

Visionox's 6.57-inch prototype had a cover glass long side of radius 3.0 mm, height 2.3 mm and angle 60°, and a short side of radius 3.4 mm, height 1.49 mm and angle 40°. By changing materials and patterns, the wrinkle margin after reliability testing was raised to 0.22 mm, 29.4% higher than the conventional method. The drawbacks were also reported. Low-modulus OCA is highly effective against wrinkles but recovers under high temperature and humidity, and patterning the panel produced OCA bubbles after reliability testing.

The main problem in curved lamination is pressure imbalance between the curved and flat portions. In the description of FIG. 12 of a Samsung Display patent, pushing the deformable pad up about 7.6 mm to apply a contact pressure of 0.1 MPa to the curved portion gives 0.96 MPa at the flat portion, about 10 times the curved portion. Putting a core inside the pad reduces the stroke to about 6.9 mm and lowers the flat-portion pressure to 0.78 MPa. These are values from the patent description, not production-line settings.

BOE wrote that conventional vacuum lamination handles covers with arcs of only 30 to 40° and proposed a jig with a 90 to 180° arc. If the OCA touches the edge of a curved cover first, it cannot be peeled off and reattached, leaving bubbles and wrinkles. Another BOE filing separated where defects occur. At the bend, insufficient pressure causes bubbles; at the transition between the flat portion and the bend, uneven stress causes cracks.

The principle for avoiding bubbles is simple. A Samsung Display filing states that the panel and window must be bonded progressively from the center to the edges so that no air layer is trapped. Another filing pre-bends the panel edges so that bubbles escape more easily. Pressing with an elastic sheet can shift the panel as the sheet stretches, misaligning it with the window. Another Visionox paper reported that OCA flows and overflows at the edges of the arc region, and concluded that the farther the OCA edge is pulled inward, the fewer the wrinkles.

The adhesive layer also varies by location. An LG Display patent describes a structure with 100 to 300 μm at the flat portion thinning to 20 to 100 μm at the curved end. No published values were found for vacuum level, production pressure or bubble defect rates in curved OLED lamination.

## Cold Forming at Room Temperature

Cold forming bends a flat sheet that has already been strengthened at room temperature and bonds it to a frame. A Corning patent states that the sheet returns flat when removed from the frame. The glass has not been deformed; the adhesive and frame are holding it bent. In Corning's patents, cold bending means below the glass transition temperature and is not limited to room temperature, but product descriptions state room-temperature bending.

Numbers follow directly. Bending a sheet produces a surface bending stress of E/(1−ν²)·(t/2)·(1/R). With Corning glass values of E 76.3 GPa and ν 0.211, bending 1.1 mm to R350 gives 125 MPa and 0.7 mm to R250 gives 112 MPa, matching the patent values. By the same formula, bending 0.5 mm glass to R10, a smartphone curvature, gives about 2,000 MPa. That is why phone curves cannot be cold-formed. The calculations in this section follow this formula.

| Thickness | R20 | R100 | R350 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.4 mm | 799 | 160 | 46 |
| 0.55 mm | 1,098 | 220 | 63 |
| 0.7 mm | 1,397 | 279 | 80 |
| 1.1 mm | 2,196 | 439 | 125 |

The table is in MPa and is calculated from the formula above. The surface compression of the Corning specimen discussed below is about 1,000 MPa, so the left column uses up or exceeds the compression. A Schott patent also states, in a non-strengthened context, that bending 0.5 mm glass to R50 gives a static stress of about 375 MPa, far above the fracture strength of ordinary glass of about 100 MPa.

The claimed ranges vary widely between documents. Corning patents respectively claimed curvature radii of 20 mm or more, more than 30 mm and less than 1.5 m, and 250 to 2,500 mm. The examples are much gentler: 0.55 mm chemically strengthened glass was bent on a vacuum chuck of about R1,000 and bonded with a 250 μm adhesive layer and a VHB tape frame. Bending is usually about one axis. One filing states that cold bending has been limited to single-axis curvature, and that saddle shapes, where opposite curvatures meet at a point, are restricted to large radii of 1 m or more.

Example 2 of Corning's 2018 filing shows what this bending does to the strengthened layer. 1.1 mm glass was strengthened to a surface compression of about 1,030 MPa, a depth of compression (DOC) of 165 μm and a central tension (CT) of about 55 MPa, then bent to R350. Surface compression on the concave face rises to 1,155 MPa and on the convex face falls to 905 MPa, the values obtained by adding and subtracting the bending stress of 125 MPa.

Depth of compression changes very differently. On the concave face it grows to 377 μm, but on the convex face it shrinks to 27 μm. Surface compression fell by 12%, but the depth fell to about 1/6. The point made in Part 1, that a deep compressive layer resists drops, takes on a different meaning for each face of a curved part. The convex face has to stop flaws with a single shallow layer.

The trend is even clearer in Example 1 with a shallow strengthened layer (0.7 mm, DOL about 30 μm). Reducing the radius from R500 to R250 more than 2 times increases the concave DOL, while the convex DOL drops by slightly more than 10%. The patent estimated that keeping CT below about 70 MPa at R600 or more is favorable for headform impact performance.

Bending also sets the central tension. A comparative example of the same 1.1 mm glass strengthened with an error-function profile went from CT 37 MPa to 149 MPa after bending. The example strengthened with a parabolic profile went from 55 MPa to 99 MPa. The one with lower CT after strengthening ended up higher after bending. In the 1.1 mm table, the difference between the two profiles over radii of 100 to 450 mm is 41 to 53 MPa.

So the limit of cold forming is not set by a single strength number. Another Corning patent plotted the minimum bend radius by flaw size. In freshly machined glass, 99.9% of flaws are smaller than 10.7 μm, and the patent advised against using glass with flaws over 35 μm on the convex side for most Gorilla Glass. Read from the graph, with a 35 μm flaw the minimum radius is about 525 mm for 0.7 mm glass and about 870 mm for 1.1 mm.

Holding the glass is also an issue. Corning's 2018 filing states that absorbing the difference in thermal expansion between glass and frame over a service temperature of −45 to 100 °C requires a two-part adhesive at least 3 mm wide and 0.3 mm high. In the same filing, the stress from this expansion mismatch can reach 5 to 100 times the bending stress, depending on adhesive and frame. Design for breakage is included as well. Another filing reported that a high-modulus adhesive retained most fragments on an R100 curve, and gave about 250 to 500 μm as the thickness of an anti-splinter film.

Corning said in 2019 that cold forming reduces the cost of curved parts by up to 40% compared with thermoforming. This is a company claim, and the basis of comparison was not disclosed. VIA optronics wrote that cold-formed displays have been installed in more than 4 million vehicles. No published values were found for the minimum production curvature or static fatigue life.

Bending extends into the panel. Corning's 2019 filing states that stress in the glass layers of a curved LCD causes mura, and proposed a structure in which perimeter adhesive applies a bending moment to reduce it. There is also a route that makes the support structure in the same step. Another filing presses flat glass into a curve inside an injection mold and injects the support structure directly behind it, with the surface coating applied before cold forming.

In vehicles, crash performance divides the designs. AGC's 2019 filing covers a cover joining a thin section of 0.05 to 0.8 mm and a thick section of 0.5 to 2.5 mm, noting that the center information display (CID) section needs more headform impact resistance. Corning's 2020 filing states that under the 80 g for 3 ms continuous criterion, if the yield strength of the support bracket is below about 250 MPa, there is no limit on elastic modulus within the range shown in the figure. In other words, the metal behind the glass, not the curvature, determines headform impact.

## When Is the Coating Applied?

The curved manufacturing routes also differ in the order in which coatings are applied. Corning wrote that many antireflection films cannot withstand thermoforming temperatures and so cannot be applied in the flat state, and that applying them to a curved surface afterward is much harder. This is a claim in a patent promoting cold forming, but the process difference is clear: cold forming finishes coating on the flat sheet and then bends it.

An LG Electronics filing described specifically what deteriorates when coating a curved surface. On a curved surface, coating uniformity is worse than on a flat one, causing a color difference between the flat and curved portions. So a 100 to 150 nm low-reflection layer is applied before curved forming, and potassium is diffused 30 to 50 μm through the layer. Printing has its own process. Corning's curved screen-printing patent claims registration of ±100 μm and border lines of ±50 μm by pressing the screen locally against the curved surface with a squeegee.

## Curved Plastic Panels

On the plastic side, when the surface layer is applied is even more extreme. A filing by Enflex of Taiwan points out that in the conventional process, flat hot pressing, curved hard coating and curved cutting each accumulate defects that collapse yield, and claims the order of applying hard coat, optical layers and printing on the flat sheet before hot pressing.

Continental solved it inside the mold. Its prototype unveiled in 2020 is a curved panel holding two 12.3-inch AMOLED displays. The back is a 375 μm printed film, the bulk is 2.1 mm PC, and the front is a 15 μm hard layer applied with a transfer film. Injection-compression molding, injecting the resin with the mold slightly open and then closing it, lowered the filling pressure. The hard layer is UV-cured after demolding.

Hardness and formability trade off against coating thickness. In a Covestro film patent, increasing the dry coating from 1 μm to 3 μm raised pencil hardness from B to 1H, but increasing it from 3 μm to 6 μm left hardness unchanged and only worsened formability. Unlike the coextruded sheet in Part 3, here the surface layer thickness is set with the curve in mind first.

Bending values for sheet are also published. The datasheet for Exolon AR FO, a hard-coated PC sheet, states that it can be bent to a radius of about 200 times its thickness at room temperature, and to about 20 to 30 times its thickness by drape forming after heating at 165 °C for 15 minutes. It is not an automotive cover lens product, but it shows that cold bending of plastics is also limited by a thickness ratio.

## Stress and Shape Measurement of Curved Parts

Curved parts are difficult to measure with equipment built for flat ones. Corning wrote that the mode spectrum of a curved part is not captured properly by flat-part prism-coupling instruments and appears smeared and distorted. So it separately claimed narrowing the measurement beam to 3 mm or less.

Shape measurement must be more precise than the tolerance. A 2017 master's thesis at National Taiwan University achieved 30 μm measurement repeatability on 3D curved glass by tilting a chromatic confocal probe to follow the curved profile, about 1/3 of the ±100 μm tolerance seen earlier. Drop testing simulates the curved shape another way. Corning's impact test apparatus patent bends the specimen over a curved support of 0.25 to 1.5 m radius and strikes it, reproducing the flexing of the device during a drop.

Strength tests also assume flat parts. The scope statement of ASTM C1499 limits specimens to plates with limited warp. A building glass research project at TU Dresden in Germany wrote that there is no standardized test method for curved glass, and in the 2017 study by Müller-Braun et al., the spherical glass considered measurable by double-ring testing had radii above 3,000 mm. That result is in the building glass range, three orders of magnitude from smartphone curvature.

Optics are similar. Vehicle windshields have distortion limits set by UN R43: 2 arcminutes in zone A and 6 arcminutes in zone B for M1 and N1 vehicles. This investigation found no strength or transmitted distortion standard specific to curved display covers. That does not mean none exists, only that none could be confirmed in public documents.

## Trends in Curved Displays for Smartphones and Vehicles

Curved designs in smartphones are declining. According to press reports, the Samsung Galaxy Note Edge had a curvature of 7R, and the S7 edge stepped from 35R, 9.4R, 5.4R to 3.8R from the panel center outward. Samsung confirmed that it reduced the curved glass of the S23 Ultra by 30%, and the S24 Ultra became flat. A Samsung support page attributed the change to S Pen usability.

Supply chain figures show the same trend. Utilization in JNTC's business reports was 61.30% in 2023, 45.10% in 2024 and 40.16% in 2025. These are not figures specific to 3D glass, because the item description changed to the entire cover glass product line from 2023.

On the other side, curved surfaces grew larger. Mercedes formed the 141 cm wide cover of the MBUX Hyperscreen at about 650 °C, and VIA launched a 1.5 m pillar-to-pillar cold-forming line. Curved covers are also being applied to products beyond smartphones. The front of the Apple Vision Pro is a single piece of three-dimensionally formed, laminated glass polished into an optical surface.

The Apple Watch shows another direction. The front crystal of the Series 7 is more than 50% thicker than that of the Series 6, and refractive design at the edge makes the screen appear to merge with the curve of the case. The curved surface has become a part responsible for both strength and optics. Corning and AUO also announced in 2023 that they would collaborate to expand production of large cold-formed curved modules.

## Comparison of Curved Manufacturing Routes

Comparing the four routes by the stress, dimensions and process conditions they leave in the part gives the following.

| Process | Temperature | What remains in the part | Coating stage | Process constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.5D | Room-temperature machining | No bending stress | After strengthening (general process) | Curvature limited to edges |
| 3D thermoforming | About 600 °C or above | 10 to 150 μm warp after strengthening | On the curved surface after forming | Molds, surface defects, yield |
| Cold forming | Room temperature | Bending stress, shallow compression on convex face | Flat state | Flaw size, adhesive and frame |
| Formed plastic | Resin up to 300 °C | Strain mismatch between surface layer and bulk | Flat or in-mold | Surface hardness, prototype figures only |

What they share is that the shaping process sets the conditions for the next process. Thermoforming has to build the deformation from strengthening into the mold in advance, and cold forming has to redesign the strengthening profile on the assumption of bending. Lamination, because of the double curvature at the corners, and measurement, because the measurement beam smears on curved surfaces, cannot use flat-part methods as they are.
