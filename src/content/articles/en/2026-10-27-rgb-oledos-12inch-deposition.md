From October 19 to 25, the issue the display industry covered most was OLEDoS (OLED on silicon). Equipment orders, a plant completion, and market forecasts all landed in a single week.

This column has never taken OLEDoS head-on. Until last issue it had only come up in passing, a line or two at a time. While retracing this week's coverage against primary sources, we ran into one number that shifts slightly from report to report: the price of a single evaporator Samsung Display will use for 12-inch RGB OLEDoS.

## News the Press Covered, October 19 to 25

First, Sunic System said on October 21 that it had won an additional order for OLEDoS evaporation equipment from an overseas customer. Newspim described the customer only as "global display companies," and The Elec reported that the customer and the order size were not disclosed. Neither article gave resolution figures or equipment specifications.

Second, Sunic System announced on October 22 the completion of its new plant in Pyeongtaek Brain City. The site covers 22,628 square meters with a total floor area of 15,756 square meters, and production capacity is about four times that of its Suwon headquarters. Building A received occupancy approval in July, and Building B has now been finished as well. It is a base for building Gen 8.6 OLED, OLEDoS, and perovskite equipment together.

Third, a forecast for the AR glasses market came out. TrendForce expects global shipments to grow 111.1% from 620,000 units in 2025 to 1.31 million in 2026. It forecasts growth of nearly 80% in 2027 as well. These are forecasts, not results. Meta's share is about 20%, and the four brands XREAL, Even Realities, Rokid, and RayNeo together hold 53.2%.

Fourth, the panel supply landscape of that market was reported alongside. Sony accounts for about 70% of OLEDoS supply, and Sony also supplies the panels for Apple's Vision Pro. The basis for that 70%, however, is not given in the article.

What goes into Meta's Ray-Ban Display is not OLEDoS but OmniVision's LCoS. That means the leader with a 20% share does not yet use OLEDoS. But SeeYA is reported to be the panel supplier for Meta's next-generation product, so if that switch happens, the size of OLEDoS demand changes in a single step.

## One Evaporator Is Half the Investment

Samsung Display's investment in 12-inch RGB OLEDoS was reported in mid-September. It is not this week's news, but this week's equipment news all points toward that investment, so we look at them together.

Here is what The Elec reported. The investment is an industry estimate of ₩300 billion, and capacity is 3,000 wafers per month on an input basis. The Asan A2 plant, which already houses a 12-inch white OLEDoS line, is the likely site, and the company is considering moving in equipment in 2027 and starting mass production in 2028. The equipment order is expected in the fourth quarter.

**The price of a single evaporator is about ₩150 billion. That is half the investment.** A typical OLEDoS evaporator costs ₩40 billion to ₩50 billion, so this is about three times as much. The remaining ₩150 billion has to cover encapsulation, inspection, cleaning, and logistics in full.

It is rare for a single equipment item to take half of an investment. So we followed what that threefold price is paying for.

## No Mass-Production RGB OLEDoS Evaporator Exists Yet

Following it, the first thing we ran into was not performance but existence.

At SID in June this year, Canon Tokki presented an RGB OLEDoS deposition system for 12-inch wafers. It uses an electroformed fine metal mask of iron-nickel alloy made by electrodeposition, holds the wafer without contact on a magnetic levitation stage, and reads alignment marks on the back of the wafer through the silicon with an infrared camera. With that, it achieved ±1 micrometer alignment and built a 3,200 PPI demo display.

TechTimes, covering that presentation, wrote two sentences side by side. It was "a demonstration system, not production equipment," and **"no production-scale RGB OLEDoS deposition system currently exists."**

What Samsung Display plans to bring in during 2027 for ₩150 billion, in other words, is a kind of equipment no one has yet run in mass production.

## The Alignment Requirement Tightens 100-Fold

Why such equipment does not exist yet comes down to one number.

In August, ZDNet Korea described the difficulty of the RGB method this way: the substrate-to-mask alignment requirement goes from about 10 micrometers for the white method to about 0.1 micrometer. That is 100 times.

White OLEDoS lays a white emitting layer on a silicon substrate and covers it with red, green, and blue color filters. Because the color filters do the work of making color, the mask does not need to line up pixel by pixel during deposition. A tolerance of 10 micrometers is enough. This is the method used by the XR devices on the market today, including Apple's Vision Pro.

RGB OLEDoS deposits each color's emitting layer directly onto its own subpixel. The mask openings have to land on those subpixel positions.

Measure how small those positions are, and the reason for 100 times appears. At the 3,200 PPI of Canon Tokki's demo, the pixel pitch is 25.4 millimeters divided by 3,200, or 7.94 micrometers, and if the three RGB subpixels sit side by side horizontally, one subpixel is 2.65 micrometers.

**Where a subpixel is 2.65 micrometers wide, a 10-micrometer error is a distance that skips nearly four cells.** It is unusable.

## At 3 Micrometers per Subpixel, Metal Masks Fail

This limit had already been documented 10 years ago.

At SID in 2016, eMagin presented a 2,645 PPI full-color OLED microdisplay (Ghosh et al., Digest vol. 47, pp. 837-840). The paper described its achievement this way: it had directly patterned "RGB subpixels as small as 3.2 micrometers x 9.6 micrometers at a 9.6-micrometer pixel pitch, without the use of fine metal masks."

And it wrote down why it had to. Fine metal masks cannot handle the roughly 3-micrometer subpixels of AMOLED microdisplays, so manufacturers had little choice other than putting color filters on white OLED.

The paper's numbers agree with one another. The pixel pitch of 2,645 PPI works out to 25.4 divided by 2,645, or 9.603 micrometers, matching the paper's 9.6 micrometers. The 3.2-micrometer subpixel width is that pitch divided by three. Subtract the 0.75-micrometer gap between subpixels that the paper states, both horizontally and vertically, and compute the area ratio, and you get 70.6%; the aperture ratio the paper reports is 70.5%.

eMagin deposited the common hole injection and transport layers all at once through an open shadow mask, and patterned only the emitting layers color by color. It cut the layers that need fine patterning to a minimum.

Samsung Display acquired that same eMagin in 2023 for about ₩290 billion. eMagin's technology page now says it is supported by Samsung Display's global resources and technical collaboration, and lists its granted patents as 56 in the U.S. and 36 abroad. As the brightness its direct patterning technology dPd can reach, it cites 28,000 candelas per square meter.

## A 1-Degree Swing Shifts the Edge by 0.21 Micrometers

What the 0.1-micrometer requirement actually means becomes clear once it is measured through thermal expansion.

The mask and the substrate are different materials. The Invar (Fe-36Ni) of a fine metal mask has a coefficient of thermal expansion of about 1.2 ppm/K at room temperature, and silicon about 2.6 ppm/K. That low value is precisely why Invar is used, but it is not the same as silicon. A difference of 1.4 ppm/K remains.

A 300-millimeter wafer has a radius of 150 millimeters. Align the two plates at the center and change the temperature by 1 degree, and the relative displacement that opens up at the edge is 150 millimeters times 1.4 ppm, or 0.21 micrometers.

**To hold 0.1 micrometer, the temperature has to be held within 0.48 degrees.** Deposition is a process that heats organic material and sprays it out, and that heat is inside the chamber.

Do the same calculation with SUS 304 and the difference is 14.7 ppm/K, so 1 degree shifts the edge by 2.21 micrometers. That is 83% of the 2.65-micrometer subpixel at 3,200 PPI. This is where the reason the mask material has to be Invar comes from.

## Canon Tokki Levitates the Wafer and Looks at Its Back

Seen this way, every part of Canon Tokki's equipment design aims at the same spot. TechTimes wrote that the equipment attacks this problem with "three integrated mechanisms": a magnetic levitation alignment stage, infrared camera registration, and a tracking mechanism that follows mask tilt.

The magnetic levitation stage holds the wafer without mechanical contact. With no contact surface, there are fewer paths for heat to travel. Reading the back-side marks with an infrared camera makes it possible to check alignment even while the front is covered by the mask. It exploits the fact that silicon transmits infrared light. Tilt tracking works together with a light-contact alignment method.

Reduce contact, watch the alignment in real time, and follow even the tilt. The ±1 micrometer result is those three combined.

And that ±1 micrometer is 10 times away from 0.1 micrometer. That is the distance left between demo and mass production.

## The Path eMagin Took Does Not Cross That Distance

Look again at eMagin's choice and it is of a different kind.

dPd does not use a metal mask. It uses a stencil made on a silicon wafer. If the mask and the substrate are the same material, the difference in their thermal expansion coefficients is zero. The 0.21 micrometers calculated above disappears. There is no longer any need to hold the temperature within 0.48 degrees.

There is one more gain. The same TechTimes article wrote that the silicon mask approach achieves **"a tighter absolute aperture tolerance than electroforming can currently match."** On top of erasing thermal expansion, the openings themselves are made more precisely.

This path does not get past the problem by pushing alignment precision higher; it removes the cause of misalignment. That is why the 2016 paper opened with "without the use of fine metal masks."

However, **what mask Samsung Display will use for 12-inch RGB OLEDoS is not stated in any public material.** We could not confirm whether it will use a fine metal mask or the silicon stencil approach that came with eMagin, even though the evaporator design changes completely depending on the answer. The ₩150 billion figure is a reported estimate, and which kind of equipment it refers to is confirmed neither by filings nor by company announcements.

## The Two Companies Swap Places Depending on the Substrate

The equipment-maker landscape is the reverse of what we see on glass.

Canon Tokki has effectively held a monopoly on the evaporator market for Gen 6 and smaller small-to-medium OLED. On the Gen 8.6 glass covered last issue, Sunic System carved out a place outside that monopoly by supplying evaporators to BOE.

On silicon wafers the order is different. TechTimes wrote that Sunic System holds more than 50% of the micro OLED evaporation equipment market and, in silicon-wafer OLEDoS, **"got there first and has held the field."** Canon Tokki is the challenger there. It was only in the first half of this year that Canon Tokki said it had developed an RGB OLEDoS evaporator.

Sunic System's track record came from China. In September, CEO Kim Hye-dong said the company had supplied a cumulative 10 or more mass-production OLEDoS evaporators for 12-inch wafers to five or more customers. The Chinese mass-production customers that can be confirmed through filings and business reports number four: BOE affiliate Chuangyuan Technology, Goerpixel, Anhui Hongxi Weixian, and SeeYA. That article does not explain the gap between five and four either.

Of these, SeeYA and BOE rank near the top of the OLEDoS panel market. A Korean equipment maker has laid down equipment across China's OLEDoS ecosystem.

Samsung Display, by contrast, has received only research evaporators and no mass-production deliveries. The Elec reported that Samsung Display is said to have chosen Sunic System's equipment after a comparative evaluation against Canon Tokki's early this year. This is a press report, not a filing. The sequence would be winning a domestic panel maker's first mass-production order on the strength of a mass-production record built in China.

## Glass and Wafers Built Under One Roof

The Pyeongtaek Brain City plant completed this week is a base that builds equipment for Gen 8.6 OLED, OLEDoS, and perovskites together. With a total floor area of 15,756 square meters, its capacity is about four times that of the Suwon headquarters.

The first two handle substrates of entirely different sizes.

Gen 8.6 mother glass measures 2,290 millimeters by 2,620 millimeters, an area of 6.00 square meters. A 12-inch wafer is a circle with a 150-millimeter radius, so 0.0707 square meters. One sheet of mother glass is 85 times one wafer.

Convert the 3,000 wafers per month of Samsung Display's RGB OLEDoS line into area and you get 212 square meters. In Gen 8.6 mother glass, that is the equivalent of loading 35 sheets a month. Set against the sheet counts a glass line handles each month, it is a nearly invisible scale in area terms.

Yet the investment is ₩300 billion and one evaporator is ₩150 billion. Value here is not priced by area. When we covered FLiPP last issue, what we counted was how many panels can be cut from one sheet of mother glass. Whether panel utilization was 75% or 93% decided the money. On the wafer side that calculation does not apply. Everything comes down to how densely pixels can be drawn into 0.0707 square meters.

Chamber size, the way the substrate is moved, the structure that holds the mask: none of these can be the same in the two machines. One has to withstand a 6-square-meter sheet of glass sagging under its own weight; the other has to hold 0.1 micrometer on a palm-sized wafer. That the same company will build both at one base is what this completion is about.

## The Filings Say the Final 10% Was Never Received

Follow that Chinese track record through the filings and a different number comes out.

On September 15 and September 30, Sunic System filed corrections to two OLEDoS evaporation equipment supply contracts. Both corrections cut the contract amount.

The two contracts' end dates were September 30 and September 15, respectively. The balances appear to have been settled to match the end dates, and the settlement was the exclusion of the last 10%. The amounts dropped from the two contracts add up to ₩6,162,060,000.

## What Remains

There are three things we tried to confirm but could not find.

First, Samsung Display's mask method. The evaporator's alignment design differs depending on whether it is a fine metal mask or a silicon stencil, but there is no public material.

Second, the primary source for the 0.1-micrometer requirement. It is confirmed only through a press report relaying an industry statement. The strictest value published from actual measurement is Canon Tokki's ±1 micrometer, and that too is a demo.

Third, the size and customer of this week's October 21 order. The company did not disclose them. It is worth noting, though, that the threshold for showing up in filings itself rose this year. Filings state the contract amount against recent revenue, and for the Anhui Hongxi contract in February that basis was 2024 revenue of ₩112.9 billion, putting the share at 18%. Now that 2025 revenue is confirmed at ₩515,700,000,000, the same ₩20.6 billion contract would sink to a 4% share. A size that would have stood out last year may not stand out this year.

One more thing: Samsung Display started mass production of white OLEDoS last November. Reports at the time said it used Canon Tokki equipment modified for OLEDoS and that yield was a little over 30%. Most of the initial volume for Samsung Electronics' Galaxy XR also came from Sony. Yields since then have not been disclosed. If the color filter method started at that level, the early yield of the RGB method, where the alignment requirement tightens 100-fold, has to be estimated separately.

## Outlook

The October forecast that AR glasses shipments will double sits side by side with the September filings showing that the last 10% of equipment payments never came in. Both are facts. The forecast speaks of 1.31 million units in 2026; the filings speak of two contracts that ended in September 2026.

A mass-production RGB OLEDoS evaporator does not yet exist. The most advanced published measurement is a demo's ±1 micrometer, 10 times away from the requirement. The ways to close that distance split into two paths. Canon Tokki raises alignment by reducing contact and looking at the back side; eMagin erases the cause of misalignment by switching the mask to silicon.

Samsung Display bought the second path in 2023 for ₩290 billion, and it has not said which path the ₩150 billion machine it plans to bring in during 2027 will take. **The moment it becomes public which kind that machine is, the mass-production prospects of 12-inch RGB OLEDoS can be weighed in numbers for the first time.**
