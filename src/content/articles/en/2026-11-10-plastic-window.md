Through Episode 2 we followed the glass row of the map. This episode takes the plastic row. Plastic covers
come in two kinds. Millimeter-thick plates of acrylic (PMMA) and polycarbonate (PC) serve the flat and
automotive cells, and films tens of micrometers thick, made of colorless polyimide (CPI), serve the folding
cell.

Episode 2 ended with glass leaving scratches to its coatings in the end. Plastic starts from the opposite side:
it does not break, but it is soft from the outset. This episode puts numbers on what PC gives up for not
breaking, how the industry splits up that bill, and how folding films trade color against fold marks.

## What PC gives up for not breaking

In notched impact, PC withstands about 30 times what PMMA does, but its surface is softer than PMMA's and it
splits light in two. The Covestro datasheet for Makrolon 2405 lists a notched Charpy impact of 65kJ/m² at 23℃.
Arkema's PMMA, Altuglas V825T, is at 2.0kJ/m².

Cold weakens PC as well. In the same datasheet, notched impact at −30℃ falls to 14kJ/m², and the specimens
break completely. That is still seven times room-temperature PMMA.

  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/impact-surface.svg" alt="A comparison of impact and surface hardness for PMMA and PC. On the right, Rockwell M hardness is 94 to 102 for PMMA and 70 to 90 for PC. Below is a sentence from a Mitsubishi Gas Chemical patent stating that the pencil hardness of PC resin only reaches about 2B." />
  <figcaption>PC wins on impact and PMMA wins on surface. One material cannot have both.</figcaption>
</figure>

On the surface, the order reverses. The Kuraray catalogue compares Rockwell M hardness at 94-102 for its own
PMMA and 70-90 for PC. In a 2010 filing, Mitsubishi Gas Chemical (MGC) wrote that the pencil hardness of PC
resin only reaches about 2B. This surface is the reason Part 2 had to put a hard coat on plastic.

Plastic also gives up a little light. Transmittance is 92% for PMMA (Röhm PLEXIGLAS 8N) and 89% for PC (1-2mm
thick). With refractive indices of 1.49 and 1.585, the calculated reflection from one uncoated face is 3.9% for
PMMA and 5.1% for PC.

  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/properties.svg" alt="Five rows of bars comparing the properties of Victus 2 glass, PMMA and PC.   <figcaption>Plastic weighs half as much as glass, but bends 24-33 times more easily and expands 11-14 times more with heat.</figcaption>
</figure>

Against glass, the cost is plainer still. A density of 1.19-1.20 is half that of Victus 2 glass at 2.41, but a
modulus of 2.4-3.3GPa is around 1/30 of glass at 79GPa. Matching the stiffness takes a thicker plate.

Heat is another weakness. The glass transition temperature is 117℃ for PMMA and 144℃ for PC, and the heat
deflection temperature under a 1.8MPa load is 98℃ and 124℃.

The MGC automotive front plate test we come to later runs at 85℃. Measured against that temperature, the
margin to heat deflection is 13℃ for PMMA and 39℃ for PC. That margin is one of the reasons the body of the
automotive front plate moved from PMMA to PC. Thermal expansion for PC is 6.5×10⁻⁵ per kelvin, about 11 times
that of Victus 2 (5.88×10⁻⁶).

Cycling through an automotive interior range of −30℃ to 85℃, a swing of 115℃, a 300mm cover grows and shrinks
by about 2.2mm in PC and about 0.2mm in glass. These values are calculated from the expansion coefficients in
the datasheets.

## A hard skin on a tough core

So the two materials are layered. In a 2016 filing, MGC wrote that automotive LCD front plates were originally
PMMA and that PC sheet came into use afterward for impact and heat resistance. What is widely used now is a
plate with an acrylic layer on PC and a hard coat on top of that.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/coextruded-plate.svg" alt="A 3D cross section of a coextruded front plate with its warpage conditions. From the top: a hard coat of 1 to 40 micrometers, a hard acrylic layer of 60 to 150 micrometers, and a PC core making up 75 to 99 percent of a total 0.5 to 3.5 millimeters. The acrylic layer handles scratches and the PC handles impact. On the right is a plate warped by moisture and heat with four conditions: a warpage change of 1,000 micrometers or less after 120 hours at 85 degrees and 85 percent humidity; a glass transition difference between skin and PC of minus 10 to plus 40 degrees; a skin resin with 65 to 80 mol percent acrylic units and at least 20 mol percent aliphatic vinyl units; and carbon dioxide release of 500 nanoliters per cubic centimeter or less so that optical bonding does not form bubbles." />
  <figcaption>The skin handles scratches and the core handles impact. What the two layers fight over is moisture and heat.</figcaption>
</figure>

The structure in MGC's 2017 filing coextrudes an acrylic layer of hardness HB-3H onto the PC core and adds a
hard coat on top. The whole plate is 0.5-3.5mm, the acrylic layer is preferably 60-150μm, and PC takes up
75-99% of the thickness. The worked examples reached pencil hardness of 2H and 3H.

Example 1 of the 2016 filing was 1.2mm overall with a 60μm acrylic layer. The hard coat is stated as preferably
1-40μm. The materials of the hard coat and how it is cured were covered in the Part 2 HC episode.

The two layers fight over moisture and heat. At 23℃ and 50% relative humidity, water absorption is 0.30% for
PMMA (Altuglas V825T) and 0.12% for PC (Makrolon 2405), a 2.5-fold difference. Kuraray writes that under
ordinary conditions PMMA takes two to three months to take up its full amount of water.

If only the thin acrylic layer absorbs extra water and swells, the PC that makes up most of the thickness has
to resist it, and the plate warps.

MGC's 2016 filing said prior art did not hold warpage well above 40℃, and claimed a warpage change of 1,000μm
or less after 120 hours at 85℃ and 85% relative humidity. A comparative example with an acrylic layer of methyl
methacrylate resin had poor shape stability.

One branch of the 2017 filing changed the skin resin to a copolymer of 65-80 mol% (meth)acrylate ester units
plus at least 20 mol% aliphatic vinyl units, made by saturating the benzene ring of styrene with hydrogen. It
said that keeping acrylic units at 80 mol% or less reduces warpage from water uptake.

The same filing goes as far as bending this plate into a curve. It gives thermoforming at 100-200℃ as
preferable, and the worked examples pressed the plate at 0.6MPa onto aluminum molds heated to 124℃ and 143℃ to
form curves of 50mm and 100mm radius, then checked for cracks.

  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/thermoform-window.svg" alt="The thermoforming window of a coextruded plate.   <figcaption>The glass transition difference between skin and core is bounded on both sides (MGC US11370206B2).</figcaption>
</figure>

This is where the glass transition difference between skin and PC gets fenced in on both sides. The claimed
range is −10℃ to +40℃. If the skin is too low, it softens like rubber at forming temperature and moves, and the
heavily crosslinked hard coat cannot follow and cracks. If it is too high, the viscosity difference between the
two resins grows, the interface roughens and flow marks appear.

Bonding the plate directly to the screen brings a different problem. In a 2010 filing, Sumitomo Chemical wrote
that gas released from the laminate forms bubbles at the bonded surface, and limited the carbon dioxide released
by heating at 80℃ for 3 hours to 500nL or less per 1cm³ of plate volume. For warpage, it said a three-layer
build with acrylic on both faces of the PC is better.

The ranges this filing cites as preferable are an acrylic layer of 140-200μm and a total of 0.4-1.5mm. A worked
example that dried the plate in vacuum brought carbon dioxide down to 300nL/cm³ or less. One more step gets
added: removing the gas in the plate before bonding.

## Why a screen goes dark through polarized sunglasses

Light leaves an LCD polarized in one direction, and if polarized sunglasses block that direction, the screen
looks black. This is exactly what happens in a car whose driver wears polarized sunglasses. If the front plate
between them rotates the polarization of the light, the screen becomes visible again.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/sunglasses.svg" alt="Three cases of viewing an LCD through polarized sunglasses. Each panel has the LCD at the bottom, the front plate in the middle and the sunglasses on top. With no birefringent front plate, sunglasses that pass only vertical polarization block the horizontally polarized light of the LCD and the screen is black. With an ordinary PC front plate of 1,000 to 3,000 nanometers retardation, the screen looks mottled with color. With PC of 3,000 nanometers retardation or more, the screen is uniformly about half as bright." />
  <figcaption>With no front plate the screen is black, with ordinary PC it is mottled, and with higher-retardation PC it is even.</figcaption>
</figure>

PC does this by nature. Stress left over from molding gives it large birefringence, which splits light into
two paths. A Covestro document lists the photoelastic constant of PC as about 84×10⁻¹²/Pa. In PC 1.5mm thick, a
retardation of 547nm corresponds to a stress of 4.3MPa.

The trouble is that the amount is uneven. MGC's 2010 filing wrote that the retardation of ordinary PC sheet is
usually 1,000-3,000nm, with nonuniformity across the sheet (P-V) of 200-500nm. Between two polarizers, this
much retardation produces rainbow-like interference colors.

For front plates on TN panels, that filing first took another path. TN panels emit light polarized at 45°. If
the optical axis of the PC sheet is set parallel or perpendicular to that polarization, the light passes through
unchanged instead of turning elliptically polarized, and no wavelength-dependent color appears. The filing said
an axis within ±10° of 45° looks fine to the eye.

Aligning the axis works when the polarization direction of the panel is fixed and the sheet can be cut to it.
The automotive filing dealt with the amount rather than the axis.

MGC's 2016 filing raised the retardation rather than reducing it. It set the front plate retardation at 3,000nm
or more, writing that below 3,000nm blackout is not sufficiently prevented. It capped the upper end, saying that
at 15,000nm or more, deformation grows in harsh environments such as the inside of a car.

Apart from one listed value (1,700nm), the retardation of the worked examples was 3,500-6,700nm. They were
evaluated by showing full white on the LCD and viewing it through polarized sunglasses, and comparative examples
below 3,000nm scored poorly.

The same idea exists inside the LCD. In a 2010 filing, Toyobo raised the retardation of the PET film protecting
the polarizer to 3,000-30,000nm to eliminate rainbow mura at oblique viewing. PET, like PC, is a resin with large
birefringence.

Toyobo attached one condition. The backlight has to be a white LED with a continuous spectrum, and the filing
said the effect does not appear with cold-cathode fluorescent lamps, whose line spectra are discrete. Raising
retardation only averages out when many wavelengths are mixed.

Calculation shows why more retardation works. Between crossed polarizers, transmittance rises and falls with
retardation at each wavelength. When retardation is large, this ripple repeats many times across the visible
range, and the eye sees the average: white light at half brightness.

In the simulation below, with retardation across the plate varying by ±250nm, a 2,000nm plate is mottled at
38-63% brightness through the sunglasses and its color varies too. A 3,500nm plate is even at 49-50% and nearly
white.

## The headform impact test in the car interior

A screen in a car sits where a head hits in a crash, so materials that do not shatter came first. The US vehicle
safety standard FMVSS 201 drives a headform 165mm in diameter weighing 6.8kg into the instrument panel at 24km/h.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/headform.svg" alt="A schematic of the headform impact test in a car interior. A headform 165 millimeters in diameter and weighing 6.8 kilograms strikes a display cover lens on the instrument panel at 24 kilometers per hour. On the right are the two conditions of FMVSS 201 S5.1. S5.1(a) is 24 kilometers per hour for most vehicles and S5.1(b) is 19 kilometers per hour for vehicles with airbags. In both cases, deceleration must not stay above 80 g for more than 3 milliseconds. Below is a note that a Corning patent tests by striking a glass cover with a 6.8 kilogram impactor at 5.35 to 6.69 meters per second." />
  <figcaption>The instrument panel head impact conditions of FMVSS 201 S5.1. Speed depends on whether airbags are fitted, and the deceleration ceiling is the same.</figcaption>
</figure>

Cars with airbags are tested at 19km/h. In either case, the deceleration of the headform must not stay above 80g
for more than 3 milliseconds. Röhm lists instrument panel covers and displays as applications in its PLEXIGLAS 8N
datasheet.

Glass is after this spot too. In a 2018 filing, Corning wrote that glass is being adopted for automotive interior
covers because of its scratch resistance and optical properties. For a 6.8kg impactor striking at 5.35-6.69m/s,
one aspect of the specification addressed deceleration of 120g or less, and the granted claims addressed the size
of broken fragments. The cold-formed glass on the Episode 1 map is the rival in this cell.

Glass carries one more condition. The same filing separately defined visibility after breakage. Even if the cover
breaks, the screen or icons beneath have to remain visible up to 15° from the front, which requires relatively
large or few fragments.

The same filing said that glass with higher surface compression is more likely to survive the headform test. The
compression budget from Episode 1 is, in effect, given a different test in the car than in the phone. PC, which
does not shatter, sidesteps both conditions from the start.

Plastic runs into the same birefringence problem again in molding. In 2020, Covestro built a prototype display over 1m long with
Dongfeng Yanfeng. It was three layers, PC and a PC blend stacked under a top film, and Covestro said it used
injection-compression and two-shot molding to reduce internal stress and birefringence.

## Why polyimide is yellow

From here on we are in the folding cell of the same plastic row. CPI is the material the first foldable phones of
2019 used on top. The tide turned when the Galaxy Z Flip adopted ultra-thin strengthened glass (UTG) in 2020, and
TrendForce counted UTG's share among the latest foldable models at over 90% in late 2023. UTG is now the mainstream
in foldable phones.

CPI has not vanished. Kolon's CPI was reported as supplied for a Xiaomi foldable phone in 2021, and it was also used
in a Lenovo foldable PC. TrendForce expected CPI to remain in use in mid-sized foldables because of mass production
technology and cost. How the switch happened, who the suppliers are, and the hard coats applied over CPI were covered
in the Part 2 flexible HC episode. Here we look at the film beneath.

The yellow of polyimide comes from charge transfer interactions between monomers. Polyimide is a polymer made by
alternating two monomers, a dianhydride and a diamine. When electron-donating and electron-accepting units overlap
closely, they absorb blue and violet light, and the light that remains looks yellow.

  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/color-cut.svg" alt="A comparison of yellow polyimide and CPI. On the left is a 25 micrometer Kapton-type film of PMDA and ODA, deep amber, with transmittance of 0.05 percent at 440 nanometers, a 50 percent cutoff wavelength of 514 nanometers, a yellowness index of 91.7 and thermal expansion of 26.0 ppm. On the right is a 50 micrometer CPI of 6FDA, BPDA and TFMB, nearly colorless, with transmittance of 87.5 percent at 440 nanometers, a cutoff wavelength of 393 nanometers, a yellowness index of 2.46 and thermal expansion of 21.7 ppm. The two cutoff wavelengths are marked on a visible-light band below." />
  <figcaption>The Kapton type blocks below 514nm, and CPI passes light down to 393nm (Kolon US9243119B2 Tables 1·2).</figcaption>
</figure>

A 2007 filing from Kolon Industries measured the two extremes side by side. A 25μm film of the same PMDA·ODA
combination as Kapton had a transmittance of 0.05% at 440nm, a wavelength of 514nm at which half the light is blocked,
and a yellowness index (YI) of 91.7.

A 50μm film made from the fluorine-containing dianhydrides 6FDA and BPDA and the diamine TFMB had a transmittance of
87.5% at 440nm, a cutoff wavelength of 393nm and a yellowness index of 2.46. Twice as thick, it still passed nearly
all the blue light.

The way to remove color is to weaken charge transfer. Bulky trifluoromethyl groups can push the chains apart, or
alicyclic rings can replace aromatic rings to make electron exchange harder. Hasegawa's 2017 paper explains that this
coloration arises from charge transfer interactions and compares many combinations.

Thickness changes color as well. In the same Kolon filing, the yellowness index of a 6FDA·4-BDAF film was 9.7 at 25μm,
11.2 at 50μm and 23.4 at 100μm. That most worked examples for cover CPI sit at 50μm is a compromise within that range.

## The price of removing color

The flat, rigid structure that makes the color also delivers low thermal expansion and high stiffness. In the tables
of Hasegawa's paper, PMDA·TFMB is deeply colored but has a thermal expansion of −5ppm per kelvin, barely expanding at
all. Using 6FDA with the same diamine makes the film colorless, but thermal expansion rises to 53-64ppm.

| Combination (Hasegawa 2017) | Color | Thermal expansion (ppm/K) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| PMDA / TFMB | Deeply colored | −5 | |
| 6FDA / TFMB | Colorless | 53-64 | Depends on imidization route |
| CBDA / TFMB | Colorless | 21 | Elongation under 10%, insoluble |
| CBDA 70·6FDA 30 / AB-TFMB | Transmittance 80.6% (400nm), YI 2.5 | 7.3 | Elongation 30% or more |

The paper notes that once such polyimides fall below about 20ppm/K in thermal expansion, they tend to lose
transparency and solubility together. As the last row shows, there are examples of mixing monomers to get over that
wall, but the basic direction is a trade-off.

The CPI in the Kolon filing had a thermal expansion of 21.7ppm, actually lower than the Kapton type (26.0ppm). It is a
combination with 20% of the flat BPDA mixed into 6FDA. Mixing in flat monomers is a choice that also takes on the risk
of color coming back.

Replacing 30 percent of the dianhydride with PMDA gives a yellowness index of 53 and retardation of 1,800 nanometers, and replacing 70 percent gives a yellowness index of 80 and retardation of 2,800 nanometers." />

A 2021 filing from SK Innovation and SKIET put numbers on that risk. In a 50μm film, replacing 30% of the dianhydride
with PMDA gave a yellowness index of 53 and a thickness-direction retardation (Rth) of 1,800nm, and replacing 70% gave
a yellowness index of 80 and 2,800nm.

Thickness-direction retardation is the birefringence that appears at oblique viewing. The PC front plate earlier
deliberately raised its in-plane retardation, but a foldable cover sitting over a circular polarizer is the opposite
case. Another filing from the same company sets Rth at 500nm or less and yellowness index at 3.5 or less together for
30-150μm films, and writes that large retardation produces rainbow mura.

Stiffness is recovered by a different route: polyamide-imide, which mixes in amide bonds. A 2019 filing from SKC (now
SK Microworks) set a room-temperature modulus of 6GPa or more, 5GPa or more at 60℃, and a yellowness index of 5 or less
in a 50μm film. Worked examples in SKIET's 2020 filing reached 7.3-8.5GPa with a yellowness index of 1.5-1.8 at
42-52μm.

## Two routes to a film

Color comes not only from monomers but also from baking temperature. Polyimide film is usually made by spreading a
solution of the precursor, polyamic acid, into a thin layer, drying it, then baking at 300-350℃ or higher to close the
imide rings. This is thermal imidization.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/film-routes.svg" alt="Two routes to making polyimide film. The upper route is thermal imidization, which spreads and dries a polyamic acid solution and then imidizes it by baking at 300 to 350 degrees or higher; alicyclic grades can suddenly color above 330 to 350 degrees. The lower route is chemical imidization, which dissolves a soluble polyimide already chemically imidized in solution, spreads it and dries it at 250 degrees or below. The thermal expansion of a 6FDA and TFMB film is 64 ppm by thermal imidization and 53 ppm by chemical imidization." />
  <figcaption>The route of baking at high temperature and the route of dissolving and spreading. Hasegawa named compatibility with chemical imidization as the key to transparent polyimide.</figcaption>
</figure>

Hasegawa's paper notes that alicyclic films often color suddenly above 330-350℃. So it took the route of closing the
imide rings chemically in solution, then spreading the soluble polyimide and drying it at 250℃ or below. The paper
calls compatibility with this chemical imidization the key to reaching the target.

The route also changes properties. Even for the same 6FDA·TFMB, the chemically imidized film had a thermal expansion
of 53ppm/K and the thermally imidized film 64ppm/K. The paper cites a tendency for chains to lie more in-plane when
spread from solution, but says the detailed mechanism is not clear.

Insoluble combinations cannot take this route. That is why CBDA·TFMB in the table above, colorless at a thermal
expansion of 21ppm, still struggles to become a cover material. Glass transition temperatures were 400℃ for PMDA·TFMB,
356℃ for CBDA·TFMB and 324-335℃ for 6FDA·TFMB. The datasheet values for the Kolon CPI used by a team at Kumoh National
Institute of Technology are a glass transition temperature of 300℃, thermal expansion of 30ppm/℃ and transmittance of
89%.

## Creases and time

The crease in CPI comes more from the time spent folded than from the number of folds. In 2021, the Kumoh National
Institute of Technology team wound Kolon CPI (measured at 42μm) around rods of 2-12mm diameter, held it for 10, 100 and
1,000 minutes, released it and measured the remaining curvature.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/fold-strain.svg" alt="A graph of outer-surface film strain against fold radius, with a side-view schematic of a test winding one sheet of film around a metal rod. Strain is the thickness divided by the sum of twice the radius and the thickness. Three curves for 42, 50 and 80 micrometers rise steeply as radius shrinks. The foldable phone range of radius 1 to 3 millimeters is shaded red, and the dashed line for the crease-free limit of about 0.4 percent corresponds to a radius of about 5 millimeters at 42 micrometers. On the left is a note that winding a 42 micrometer film around a 2 millimeter diameter rod gives 14,944 microstrain, about 1.5 percent." />
  <figcaption>On the left is a side view of the test that winds one sheet of film around a metal rod to measure outer-surface strain. Folding at a radius of 1-3mm stretches the film several times beyond the crease-free limit. The equation and limit are from Choi et al. 2021.</figcaption>
</figure>

The outer surface of a film wound on a rod stretches by the thickness divided by twice the radius. A 42μm film wound
on a 2mm diameter rod reaches 14,944 microstrain, about 1.5%. This value is calculated with a radius corrected for the
film lifting slightly off the rod. The researchers inferred a limit of about 4,000 microstrain (0.4%), a radius of
about 5mm, below which no residual strain remains.

The fold test radii used in foldable cover patents are 1-3mm (SKC 2 and 3mm, Samsung Display about 1mm). Fold a 50μm
film at a radius of 1.5mm and the outer-surface strain is about 1.6%, four times this limit. At the same radius,
raising the thickness to 80μm gives 2.6%. A thicker cover is stiffer, but stretches that much more in a fold. Geometry
alone cannot eliminate the crease, so the contest becomes how quickly the remaining crease recovers.

<figure class="fig-single">
  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/hold-vs-cycle.svg" alt="A comparison of two tests. On the left, film is wound on a rod, held for 10, 100 and 1,000 minutes and released; a schematic shows that the longer the hold, the more the released film stays bent. There was no meaningful change on rods of 6 and 12 millimeters diameter. On the right is a test bending film back and forth 10,000 times on a 2.3 millimeter diameter roller at 2 hertz, with no meaningful residual curvature." />
  <figcaption>The longer the film stayed wound, the more of a crease remained, and rolling it 10,000 times left none (Choi et al. 2021).</figcaption>
</figure>

The result leaned toward time. The thinner the rod and the longer the hold, the larger the residual strain. By
contrast, specimens bent back and forth 10,000 times at 2Hz on a roller of 2.3mm diameter showed no meaningful change
in curvature. Each bend lasted only a moment.

1,000 minutes is a little over 16 hours, about the time a foldable phone spends closed overnight. The researchers went
on to measure the polymer's recovery, in which the strain remaining after the load is removed declines over time.

They measured the recovery rate too. The fraction of residual strain remaining after unfolding fell along a single
line, 1 − 0.23·log t (t in minutes), regardless of how long the film had been held. Relative to the value after 1
minute, 77% remains after 10 minutes, 54% after 100 minutes and 31% after 1,000 minutes.

Extending this line as it stands, reaching zero takes about 15 days. The paper measured up to 1,000 minutes, so
anything beyond is a calculation that extends the equation. In the simulation below, you can change the thickness, the
radius and the time since unfolding.

Industry tests measure time as well. In a 2019 filing, SKC folded a 50μm film at a radius of 2mm, held it for 24 hours
at 25℃, released it and measured the inner angle. Worked examples reached 120° or more and comparative examples were
115° or less, and the claim was set at 120° or more.

A 2020 filing from Samsung Display used harsher conditions. Film was folded at a radius of about 1mm, held at 60℃ and
93% relative humidity, released, and the outer angle between the two planes was measured. 0° is fully flat.

Three single-layer polyimide comparative examples, with moduli of 6.6-7.6GPa, stayed bent at 98-128°. A worked example
bonding two 10-35μm polyamide films with a 1-5μm adhesive layer reached 90° with a modulus of 12.0GPa, and the filing
set 40-90° as the preferred range.

The filing explained that bonding several high-modulus films tightly with thin adhesive layers helps the deformation
return more easily to the original shape. In this table, however, material and structure change together, so it cannot
be read which of the two reduced the angle.

The numbers from the three tests cannot be compared directly. Even the way the angle is defined is opposite.

| Test | Radius and conditions | Measured | Flat state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kumoh 2021 | 1-6mm rods, room temperature, 10-1,000 minutes | Residual strain | 0 |
| SKC 2019 | 2mm, 25℃, 24 hours | Inner angle | Reads as 180° |
| Samsung Display 2020 | About 1mm, 60℃ and 93% humidity | Outer angle | 0° |

SKC's "120° or more" is better when larger, and Samsung Display's "90° or less" is better when smaller. Radius,
temperature and humidity, and the angle measured all differ, so the numbers from the two filings must not be placed on
one line. The same goes for the drop heights we saw in Episode 1.

## UV on the top layer

The cover takes sunlight directly at the very top. SKIET's 2020 filing ran 340nm ultraviolet (0.55W/m²) at 40℃ for 20
hours followed by 4 hours off, three times over for a total of 72 hours, then measured yellowness index again. These
are values for the film itself before the hard coat.

  <img src="/articles/2026-11-10-plastic-window/uv-yellowing.svg" alt="A bar chart of yellowness index before and after 72 hours of ultraviolet cycling. The four worked examples went from a yellowness index of 1.5-1.8 to 4.3-4.8, a rise of 2.8-3.0. The comparative examples
went from 3.0-4.0 to 8.3-11, a rise of 5.3-7. Haze also split, at 0.26-0.33% for worked examples and 0.8-1.5% for
comparative examples. Even the best film turned nearly three times yellower in three days.

The same filing also listed the chlorine content left in the film. Worked examples had 6-33ppm, and comparative examples
1 and 3, which yellowed heavily, had 48ppm and 68ppm. However, comparative example 2, at 4ppm chlorine, still rose 5.3 in
yellowness index, so chlorine alone does not explain it.

## What remains in the plastic cells

Reduced to the numbers for plates: PC is about 30 times stronger in impact than PMMA and half the weight of glass, but it
brings a surface at about 2B pencil hardness, thermal expansion about 11 times that of glass, and uneven birefringence of
1,000-3,000nm.

The industry pays those off separately.

| Cost | Number | How it is paid off |
|---|---|---|
| Soft surface | PC at about 2B pencil | Acrylic skin of 60-150μm, hard coat to reach 2H-3H |
| Warpage from moisture and heat | Absorption: PMMA 0.30%, PC 0.12% | Copolymer skin, Tg difference −10 to +40℃ |
| Uneven birefringence | 1,000-3,000nm, P-V 200-500nm | Axis alignment (TN), 3,000nm or more (automotive) |
| Bubbles at the bonded surface | Gas released on heating | CO₂ at 500nL/cm³ or less |

The evidence is MGC's 2010, 2016 and 2017 filings and Sumitomo Chemical's 2010 filing.

What these share is that the PC core stays as it is. The material that takes the impact is left alone, and the problems of
surface, interface and light are solved with layers and specifications added above and below it.

Reduced to the numbers for films: CPI became transparent by raising transmittance at 440nm from 0.05% to 87.5%, but in
return, the low thermal expansion and stiffness that the flat structure provided have to be made up some other way. Mix the
flat units back in, and yellowness index and thickness-direction retardation return together.

The manufacturing route is narrow as well. Baking at high temperature can bring the color back, so choosing a soluble
composition and spreading it at 250℃ or below was named as the key. A film that is colorless with low thermal expansion
but insoluble cannot get on that route.

In folding, the outer-surface strain of a 50μm film at a radius of 1-3mm is 0.8-2.4%, which is 2-6 times the crease-free
0.4%. So the contest is not about eliminating the crease but about how quickly a crease from a night of being folded
recovers. After 1,000 minutes, a third of it still remains. The industry sets its tests at 24 hours, or at 60℃ and 93%
humidity, to compete on that recovery, and has even brought out structures that bond several thin sheets instead of using
one.
