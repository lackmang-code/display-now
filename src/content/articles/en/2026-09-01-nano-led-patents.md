## 1. Overview

### 1.1 Background and purpose

There are broadly two ways of putting nanometre-scale light-emitting diodes onto a screen. One makes the LEDs separately on a wafer, floats them in a liquid, sprays them onto a substrate and lays them down with an electric field; the other grows them vertically in place on the substrate. The former is what Samsung Display has developed under the name QNED, the latter what France's Aledia has taken into a mass production line for augmented reality microdisplays.

The two methods are physically opposite. And yet following the bibliographic records of the granted patents turns up one thing in common. **Both lineages started at universities and national laboratories, and the patents of both lineages ended up at Samsung.** One was bought by Samsung Display, the other by Samsung Electronics. And the company now building a line and making product is the one that did not sell.

This piece confirms that configuration through bibliographic records and claims, and works out why laying them down is hard, using the numbers written into the claims.

### 1.2 Scope

12 granted patents were taken as the sample: 8 on the laying-down side, 3 on the growing-up side, and 1 as a contrast case. It is not an exhaustive survey but a sample of landmarks chosen from each camp, selected on the pipeline's five indicators (number of family countries, grant status, abandonment or lapse, divisional and continuation offspring, same-day filing clusters) and on the assignment history that is this piece's axis.

Two things were deliberately left out. One is **micro LED mass transfer.** Once device size rises above single-digit micrometres, both the forces handled and the process change, and putting it in the same piece blurs the focus. The other is **QNED's colour conversion layer (quantum dots).** This piece's axis is the problem of seating a device in its place, not the problem of changing its colour once seated.

That difference becomes a problem once later: **transfers of rights on Korean grants do not appear in Google Patents bibliographic records.** Every assignment confirmed in this piece is from the US Patent Office record on a US grant.

The numbers are all publication counts. The same invention published in Korea, the United States and China counts as three. They are not family-level counts, so they are not used to compare companies, only to look at distribution over time within one company.

### 1.4 Technical classification

This piece reads the patents on two axes. What is changed (the lever) is device structure, ink and solvent, electric field conditions, equipment and inspection, and substrate growth. What is being removed (the aim) is dispersion and injection, angle, polarity, count and yield, and colour.

Why the aim axis is drawn that way emerges in Sections 3 and 4. Spraying with ink and seating carries **three different kinds of randomness**, and the twelve sets of claims attack each of the three by different means.

## 4. Comparative analysis

### 4.1 The randomness is not one but three

The process of floating rods in a liquid, spraying them and seating them with an electric field carries three kinds of randomness, different in character. The twelve sets of claims attack these three by different means, and without separating them there is no explaining why so many patents are needed.

<div class="viz-box">
  <div class="viz-title">THREE RANDOMNESSES</div>

| | What is random | The laying-down response | The growing-up side |

### 4.2 Angle was not the problem

Angle was calculated first. A simulator showing how the force laying a rod down by dielectrophoresis contends with thermal motion. The voltage and frequency ranges the patents claim are laid directly over the physical limits.

</div>

Three values can be read at the default.

First, **the voltage at which the field ties with thermal motion is 0.011V.** That is 1/5,000 of the 60V the patents use. Apply a field on the order of 10 to the 7th volts per metre to a rod 4μm long and the alignment energy becomes ten million times the thermal energy. **Laying rods down parallel is not in itself hard.** At this size Brownian motion is no contest.

Second, the upper wall is dielectric breakdown of the solvent. At an electrode gap of 3μm it is about 120V, and the patents' upper limit of 100V (±50V) sits just below it.

Third, so the wall that actually remains is the one on the left. Push the frequency slider left and the double layer at the electrode surface screens the field, reducing what is applied inside the solution. In this model the stability line comes out at about 25kHz, and the 50kHz floor claimed by US10361246B2 lies above it while the 10kHz floor claimed by US11685154B2 lies below.

And what sets the position of this wall is the solvent. Raise the conductivity slider to 10 to the minus 2nd and the stability line rises to 252kHz, and half the claimed range disappears.

One caution here. The wall this model draws is an approximation based on double layer screening and AC electro-osmotic flow, while the 50kHz in the specification is an experimental value. **The closeness of the two numbers is not read as evidence the model is right.** That the specification's stated reason for the floor was "the number of devices mounted falls" does not, however, run against this direction. When the field weakens, what scatters is not the angle but the number caught.

And if angle really was not the problem, why did US10145518B2 put ±30 degrees into its claim? Because the calculation deals only with thermal motion. The real scatter comes from elsewhere: fluid flow, rods already seated distorting the field for the next rod, non-uniform field at the electrode edges. **What the calculation tells us is not "the alignment works well" but "the cause of the scatter is not thermal motion."**

### 4.3 What the electric field cannot decide

Even with the angle fixed, polarity remains. The force laying a rod down does not distinguish its two ends. The induced dipole aligns along the field direction, but turning that axis 180 degrees leaves the energy identical. So under a symmetric waveform, which electrode the p-type touches becomes a coin toss.

This is why US10361246B2 uses an asymmetric waveform. The waveform in the figure above does not go negative, swinging only between 0V and +30V. Its RMS value is 18.4V. Doing this creates a force biased in one direction, raising the matching proportion, and the point the specification records reaching is **60% or more**.

Turn that around and **the other 40% are lying the wrong way.** Under DC drive that 40% does not light.

### 4.4 Count is luminance

Now to the randomness of position. Spray with ink and the number landing in each pixel scatters, and only those with matching polarity light. If pixel luminance is proportional to the number lit, that scatter becomes luminance scatter directly.

<div class="sim-embed" data-sim="nanoled-count-polarity-demo" data-params='{"lambda":120,"polarity":60,"pitchUm":125}'>
</div>

Assume a Poisson distribution with the sprayed count as its mean, and keeping only those with matching polarity leaves a Poisson distribution again. So the luminance coefficient of variation is inversely proportional to the square root of the number lit. The arithmetic becomes very simple.

Put the number from the original 2011 patent into it. What claim 1 of US9059114B2 demanded was **at least five per pixel.** Apply 60 percent polarity and the average number lighting is three, and the luminance coefficient of variation is 58 percent. Pixels differ in brightness by half.

Take 3 percent as the threshold at which mura becomes visible and the required count follows.

| 5 | 3 | 57.7% | 0.03% | 1.0% |
| 100 | 60 | 12.9% | 0.5% | 20% |
| 1,000 | 600 | 4.1% | 5.1% | 200% |
| **1,852** | **1,111** | **3.0%** | **9.5%** | **370%** |

**Meeting 3 percent means spraying 1,852 per pixel.** Taking one rod as occupying 4μm by 0.6μm, in a 65-inch 4K subpixel (short side 125μm) that is 9.5 percent of the area. It fits.

Put the same number into a mobile subpixel (short side 20μm) and it is 370 percent. **It does not fit.**

The smaller the pixel, the less room to raise the count, and without raising the count the scatter grows. That this method headed for television from the start can be read not as chance but as a matter of area.

This calculation is more optimistic than reality, though. Every rod was taken to be equally bright, and neither the effect of seated rods blocking the place of the next nor the positional correlation of crowding near the electrodes was included. The real scatter is larger.

### 4.5 Grow them up and three vanish at once

Return to claim 1 of US9245948B2. The support surface is divided into regions of two materials and wires grow only in one of them.

That single sentence erases three rows of the previous section's table at once. **Position** is set by the mask, **angle** by the growth direction, **polarity** by the growth order. Lay n-type first, put the active layer on and cover with p-type, and there is no way for top and bottom to be reversed.

Other problems come instead. Lattice mismatch in growing gallium nitride on silicon, keeping wire height and diameter uniform across a whole wafer, and colour. US10424692B2 solves the last by shape. Under the same growth conditions, varying cross-sectional diameter and slope changes the amount of indium incorporated and hence the wavelength.

### 4.6 7 September 2018, and two weeks later

Back to the timeline. Within the sample, the earliest priority date among Samsung Display's own filings is US12004412B2, 21 September 2018. **Two weeks** after the assignment of 7 September.

That patent's claim sits at a different layer from those before it. Where the PSI patents dealt with device, solvent and voltage conditions, this one claims equipment applying an electric field in mid-air while the ink is falling, setting the direction in advance. And four months later US11685154B2 puts a sensing unit measuring the position of individual dipoles into an equipment claim.

**The axis moves from material and conditions to equipment and metrology.** This observation rests on a sample of twelve, though, and does not say Samsung Display made no related filings before that.

### 4.7 The one that did not sell

Glo AB's patent history holds something unlike either lineage above.

<div class="quote-box"><p>2021-08-13 assigned to Nanosys<br />2022-04-01 security interest granted with Fortress Credit Corp. as agent<br />2023-09-07 security released<br />2023-10-05 renamed Sysonan<br />current owner Samsung Electronics Co Ltd</p></div>

Even the record of a security interest being granted and released remains. Nanowire LED patents out of Lund University in Sweden changed hands several times before reaching Samsung Electronics.

Over the same period Aledia holds 193 granted patents in its own name. Several also keep the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission and the National Centre for Scientific Research as joint owners. Of three filed on a single day, 28 December 2012, FR3000613B1 lapsed for non-payment of annuities. It discards what it discards and holds the rest.

**In sum, then.** The patents out of Kookmin University went to Samsung Display, the patents out of Lund University to Samsung Electronics. The patents out of the French national laboratories were not sold. And the company that has announced a mass production line for nano LED is the one that did not sell.

This sentence is not causation. It does not mean that selling the patents is why mass production was not reached, nor the reverse. It is only the picture the bibliographic records draw.

### 4.8 After the assignment

It looks as though the story closes on 7 September 2018. It does not.

Nineteen months after the assignment, on **27 April 2020**, "micro-nanofin LED element" was filed. The applicant is not PSI but the **Kookmin University Industry-Academic Cooperation Foundation**, and the inventor is Young Rag Do. According to reports it is the product of national research and development projects including a Ministry of Science and ICT research centre programme.

What deserves attention is not the claims but **the background art in the specification**.

<div class="quote-box"><p>To overcome such difficulties, granted patent publication No. 10-1436123 discloses a display manufactured by a method of dropping a solution containing nanorod-type LEDs onto a subpixel and then forming an electric field between two alignment electrodes to self-align the nanorod-type LED elements on the electrodes, thereby forming the subpixel. However, the nanorod-type LEDs used have a small light extraction area and thus poor efficiency, so that <b>a large number of LEDs must be mounted in order to obtain the intended efficiency</b>, and the nanorod-type LEDs themselves have a high likelihood of defects.</p></div>

Where that No. 10-1436123 came from is set out in the same specification.

<div class="quote-box"><p>A detailed description of this is incorporated by reference from granted patent publications No. 10-1490758 and No. 10-1436123, <b>by the inventor of the present invention</b>.</p></div>

The background art section is written by the applicant, and **disclosing prior art is required by patent law.** So this passage means it was not concealed, and no more than that. What is worth reading is elsewhere: **what limit was named.**

That obtaining the intended efficiency requires mounting a large number. **This is where the simulator arrived in Section 4.4.** How many land in a pixel sets the luminance scatter, and the route to raising the count is blocked by subpixel area. We put that calculation together belatedly by overlaying two claims; the inventor wrote it into a specification in one sentence in 2020. This method's limit was documented from the inside before it was pointed out from the outside.

The nanofin is the answer to that sentence. Turn the rod into a plate and the light-emitting area for the same volume widens, so fewer are needed.

**This lineage continues today.** The micro-nanofin of 2020 changed its name to ultrathin fin LED from 2021 and branched into device, ink composition and electrode assembly, and the most recent filing confirmed is 19 November 2025. Among them is a method that, instead of an electric field, **aligns by fitting devices into holes cut in the same shape as the device** (KR102739686B1). The shape has no axis of symmetry, so a device flipped over will not fit, which is the gist of the claim: polarity set by geometry. The idea of seating a device floating in fluid into a matching recess is not itself new, having already been tried in micro LED assembly (US10276754B2), and **a granted right is not a running process.** This piece did not read that lineage as a sample, so it stops here.

**The current owner is the Kookmin University Industry-Academic Cooperation Foundation.** The bibliographic records of the five most recent filings were opened and checked, and there is as yet no bulk assignment record like that of 2018. Over the same period the same inventor continues to appear on filings in Samsung Display's name. Those are outside the sample so their content was not checked; only that the bibliographic records say so is recorded.

## 5. Counter-evidence and limits

**Transfers on Korean grants could not be confirmed.** Google Patents bibliographic records do not show transfers of rights on Korean grants. Every assignment confirmed in this piece is on a US grant, and when and how the Korean family of the same invention was transferred needs separate confirmation.

**The nanofin lineage was not read as a sample.** Reports put the 2020 filings at 6 while what this piece confirmed from bibliographic records is 5. The later ultrathin lineage was only swept by search, without a close reading of the claims as a sample. Section 4.8 says only that the lineage exists and continues, and makes no judgement on scale, technical superiority or manufacturability.

**The transfer consideration could not be confirmed.** A figure of 10 billion won appears in reports but could not be confirmed from primary material, so it is not used in the text.

**There was a case where a reported figure and a search figure coincided by chance.** As recorded in Section 2.2. When numbers match, failing to open the list lets a false cross-verification go straight into the article.

**No judgement is made on whether development was halted.** There are reports, but they could not be confirmed from primary material, and in the sample the 2022 priority year is the largest. The collapse from 2024 may be explained by the 18-month publication delay.

**The two simulators are models showing the principle, not measurements.** The wall in Section 4.2 is an approximation based on double layer screening and electro-osmotic flow, while the 50kHz in the specification is an experimental value. Two numbers being close does not verify the model. The coefficient of variation in Section 4.4 is an optimistic lower bound resting on the assumption that every rod is equally bright.

**What the solvent patent limits is not conductivity.** It is viscosity, boiling point and vapour pressure. The simulator's conductivity axis is a separate axis the model predicts, and is not read as what that patent aimed at.

**Chronological order is not read as causation.** That a filing came two weeks after an assignment does not mean its content came from the assigned patents. Allowing for preparation time, it is more likely work already under way before that.

**Empty cells in the sample matrix are not absence.** They mean that combination did not arise in a sample of twelve.

**No conclusion is drawn about Chinese entry.** BOE 15, Shenzhen China Star 0 and TCL 0 are the result of looking through the single search term `"dielectrophoresis"`. The possibility that Chinese applicants use different terminology cannot be excluded.

**One contrast case does not represent LG Electronics' whole approach.** One of 121 self-assembly grants was read.

## 6. Conclusions and implications

What runs through the twelve is a single choice: **at which stage to remove the randomness.**

The laying-down side makes the device first and finds its place later. So position, angle and polarity all become problems of the back end of the process, and all three have to be attacked by different means. Dispersion is made with insulating and hydrophobic films, shape makes them easy to lay down, solvent viscosity and vapour pressure are matched, an asymmetric waveform raises polarity to 60 percent, and finally a sensor counting the position of individual devices goes onto the equipment. The reason there are so many patents is not that the technology is hard but that **the problem divides into several.**

The growing-up side fixes the place first and makes the device in that place. The three randomnesses vanish at once in the growth step, and in exchange it takes on other problems: lattice mismatch and uniform growth.

And the problem of count was a problem of size. How many land in a pixel sets the luminance scatter, and how many can be seated is set by subpixel area. The 1,852 needed for 3 percent mura fits in a 65-inch television subpixel and does not fit in a mobile subpixel. That this method stayed on the large-screen side has area as its reason.

And that limit was written into a specification before it was pointed out from outside (Section 4.8). The picture this piece draws, of two lineages converging on Samsung, is a photograph stopped in 2018, and the same inventor's name has stayed on filings from both university and company since.

The next piece will cover the micro LED mass transfer deliberately left out here. Once devices get larger the forces handled change too, and an altogether different landscape appears, with Taiwanese companies as the protagonists.
